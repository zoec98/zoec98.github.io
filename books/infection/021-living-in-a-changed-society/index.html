                                                                                                                                                  Chapter 21: Living in a Changed Society

                                                                                                                                                  Living in a Changed Society#

                                                                                                                                                  Several weeks into her endeavor of trying to blend in better, Sonia found that her adjustments had begun to feel… almost normal.

                                                                                                                                                  Though initially foreign and uncomfortable, she had become accustomed to the ritual of lacing on her underbust corset before bed. What was once a task she approached with reluctance now brought an unexpected sense of security. The absence of its constrictive embrace made her feel exposed and vulnerable. The transition from her day corset, a formidable S-bend that sculpted her figure into the accepted silhouette, to the slightly more forgiving night corset had become a daily ritual.

                                                                                                                                                  It felt normal too that her hands now danced with a fluid grace that came naturally. Gone was the clumsy and jerky signaling she had used when she first picked up hand-dancing again after returning from Shanghai. The act of curtsying, a gesture she often had performed ironically when she was a maid, had woven itself into her interactions so seamlessly that its absence now felt like an oversight, a breach of an unspoken contract between her and the world she navigated. As she stood before the mirror, masked and corseted, Sonia saw her reflection staring back at her. The woman in the mirror moved with practiced elegance, her gestures refined, her posture demure.

                                                                                                                                                  She recalled a recent encounter at a bookstore where a man had reached for a volume on the top shelf for her. His actions were gentle, protective, and anticipated her needs before she could even express them. Sonia had curtsied instinctively, a silent “thank you,” and had felt a rush of gratitude for the simple act of kindness. This exchange had felt surprisingly natural, even right.

                                                                                                                                                  Similar situations happened every day, multiple times a day: in the tram, at work, out when shopping or in the café, everywhere someone, usually a man, was around her and helped her before she could even realize that she needed assistance. Her clothing, restrictive as it was, should be hindering her, but in every situation where this would be the case, some man was present in time, courteously helping her to effortlessly and elegantly navigate the situation. Doors opened before her when she was in a restrictive or voluminous skirt, chairs moved behind her when she was about to sit down, and things she could not reach materialized in her hands whenever she thought of reaching for them. Seats in the tram were offered to her, and hands of strangers helped her board the tram or disembark.

                                                                                                                                                  Men at work organized meetings and functions in a way that took women in corsets and demanding high heels into account, and thus she was able to walk wooden planks across a soft lawn in her heels on the GAIA summer party, and found convenient clusters of high-backed chairs at her disposal to rest her feet. When she intended to sit down on them, a random colleague was present to push the chair as she sat down, making her restrained movements elegant. She was bound, helpless and mute in her clothing, and it was… convenient?

                                                                                                                                                  The situation at work developed similarly, and in ways that Sonia had not anticipated. In her office at GAIA, Sonia sat on her Balans kneeling stool, which she found better suited to her narrow underskirt than any chair. Her corseted form reflected in the sleek surface of her workstation, she contemplated the paradoxes that had become her daily reality. The mask that concealed her facial expressions and the gloves that sheathed her hands—each element, initially a symbol of constraint, had seamlessly integrated into her professional life in ways she never anticipated.

                                                                                                                                                  Typing with gloved fingers was a challenge Sonia initially met with frustration. Each keystroke felt like a battle, the tactile disconnect rendering her once-fluent movements clumsy and uncertain. However, over time, she adapted, finding rhythms and methods that mitigated the barrier imposed by the gloves. The nuisance of removing them became so irritating that it had turned into a ritual she seldom performed. The sensation of being unprotected now evoked visceral discomfort, a strong dislike of her bare skin exposed to the unseen filth of the world.

                                                                                                                                                  In meetings, the dynamics had shifted dramatically. The once chaotic exchanges, mostly with men speaking over each other, had transformed into a structured ballet of gestures and allocated turns. Sonia recalled the times before the Change when she struggled to be heard—a sharp contrast to the present, where each woman, masked and silent, waited for her designated moment to contribute via hand-dancing. The men, in turn, had adapted to this regime, focusing their attention on interpreting the gestures, ensuring that the women’s inputs were not just seen but understood.

                                                                                                                                                  The enforced order, a product of the Changed’s adherence to submission and structured interaction, had paradoxically created a space where women’s contributions were guaranteed equal weight and consideration. Sonia realized that these practices, which she had viewed as regressive and antithetical to feminism, had inadvertently leveled the academic playing field. She recalled a recent project meeting where her hand-dancing suggestions were met with nods of approval and thoughtful consideration. The absence of verbal interruptions and the focused attention on her gestured input underscored a sense of professional respect she had not fully appreciated before.

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia found herself at a crossroads of thought, recognizing the efficacy and fairness these Changed practices had introduced into her work environment. The irony was not lost on her: the restrictions she resisted had fostered an unexpected form of empowerment within the academic arena. But this acknowledgment was tinged with lingering dissonance. The gains in effectiveness and equality were overshadowed by the loss of vocal expression, a trade-off that Sonia continued to grapple with every time she felt the tug of the mask and the silicone ball in her mouth.

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia was forced to reassess her understanding of feminism, effectiveness, and autonomy. The realization that freedom could take many forms, and that empowerment could emerge from unexpected sources, was a journey of discovery that challenged her preconceptions.

                                                                                                                                                  Navigating through her day, Sonia was acutely aware of the constraints her clothing imposed, and also of the weird new social status indicators the Changed had. The buttons on her dresses were at the shoulder, not at the back as with other women: she had to be able to dress alone, without assistance from a man or a maid. Her billowing and restrictive skirts made certain tasks daunting, if not impossible, without assistance. But men were often there, anticipating her needs before she could even articulate them.

                                                                                                                                                  This dependence, Sonia reflected, was not the kind of helplessness she feared but a form of interdependence that the Changed society fostered. Men, conditioned to be ever attentive and supportive, engaged with her in a non-verbal dance of gestures and anticipation. It was parallel to the hand-dancing that had become her voice, each act a thread in the fabric of a collectivist society that prized harmony and mutual support over individual autonomy.

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia struggled to find a term for the unique form of intimacy these everyday interactions bred. It was an intimacy born of necessity, a constant connection that was surprisingly comforting. The realization that she was never truly alone “out there,” that the societal structure ensured she was supported, brought both comfort and a lingering question about the nature of autonomy. The only time she was ever alone was at home, in her apartment, where the silence and stillness offered a stark contrast to the orchestrated interactions of her public life. Sonia contemplated the idea that perhaps being alone, being autonomous in the way she once cherished, was anathema to the Changed ethos. The solitude she once sought now felt like an aberration, a deviation from the collective embrace of society. Was autonomy then not about independence but about finding one’s place within the web of societal support and mutual dependency? Sonia resolved to ask Anna more and more detailed questions about “living as Changed.”

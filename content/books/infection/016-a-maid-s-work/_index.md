# A Maid's Work

Sonia's placement into Cheng's household happened faster than it should have.

That was what frightened her.

The interview lasted less than twenty minutes. Mrs. Huang looked over her papers, asked practical questions about tray service, laundering delicate fabrics, flower care, discretion, and family background, then watched her kneel to present tea without spilling a drop. Sonia kept her eyes lowered, her answers short, her accent softened where Mei Lin had coached it. The corset held her shoulders back. The narrow line of the skirt kept her steps modest. Even her fear helped. It made her careful.

Mrs. Huang took the cup from the tray, tasted it, and set it down.

"Turn."

Sonia obeyed.

"Again."

She turned back.

Mrs. Huang studied the fit of the dress, the line of Sonia's posture, the composure she kept wrapped around her own body like another layer of clothing.

"You have worked in formal households before?"

"Yes, Madam."

"And you understand that staff here are not seen unless needed?"

"Yes, Madam."

Mrs. Huang's gaze did not warm, but it settled.

"Good. We prefer women who understand the value of invisibility."

By evening Sonia had a room under the roof and a gray dress hanging from a hook beside the bed.

The room was narrower than the one she had used in training and cleaner in a way that felt less kind. Bed. Basin. Wardrobe. Small shelf. One high window that gave her a slice of sky and almost nothing else. The villa kept its beauty for the rooms that mattered. Up here the point was not beauty. It was order.

She put her bag on the bed and stood in the middle of the room for a moment with her hands hanging at her sides.

This was success. This was the closest she had come to Cheng, to proof, to the source of what had been done to the world.

It was also an attic maid's room in a house built by a man who might have engineered the end of the life she had understood as normal.

She unpacked quickly because there was nothing worth arranging. A few changes of underthings. Toiletries. Phone. Chargers. The documents that proved she was not really who the household records said she was stayed hidden where they needed to stay hidden. When she finished, the room looked exactly as impersonal as before.

In the morning the house dressed her properly.

The uniform was stricter than what Mei Lin had used in training and more anonymous. Gray from throat to hem. High collar. Long sleeves. White apron. Gloves for service. The corset underneath came with shoulder braces that discouraged softness in the spine. Beneath the dress, a narrow underskirt shortened her stride and kept her knees honest. There was no decorative charm to any of it. This was not the flirtation of boutique restraint. This was livery. A woman made orderly, serviceable, and interchangeable.

The maid in the mirror had no edges anyone needed to remember.

At roll call the women stood in a line outside the service pantry while Mrs. Huang inspected them one by one. Powder even. Hair controlled. Shoes clean. Aprons straight. Sonia held herself still and let her face go blank. Around her the other maids smelled faintly of starch, soap, and the warm press of bodies already laced and fastened for the day.

Mrs. Huang stopped in front of a younger maid and adjusted the girl's cuff with two sharp fingers.

"Crooked," she said. "Do it again and begin the morning from the start."

The girl bowed her head at once.

"Yes, Madam."

Mrs. Huang moved on.

Nothing in her voice rose. Nothing needed to. The household did not run on shouting. It ran on certainty.

That was worse.

Sonia learned the rhythm fast because she had been trained for it and because the house left no room for failure to become personal. A maid either worked or waited to work. There was no third state. She laid breakfast trays, polished silver, dusted carved screens, carried pressed linen, refreshed flowers, and stood by doors with her hands placed correctly while conversations happened past her as if she were a useful furnishing.

The anonymity had weight.

In Mei Lin's flat Sonia had still felt watched as Sonia. Here she disappeared into function. The women beside her moved with the same small steps, the same lowered gaze, the same quick economy at the wrists. The house swallowed individuality and returned efficiency.

It would have been easier to resist if it had felt uglier.

Instead it felt smooth.

One afternoon Mr. Chen stopped beside her as she finished arranging tea things in a downstairs sitting room.

"Miss Sonia."

She turned at once and lowered her eyes.

"Sir?"

He inspected the tray without touching it. Mr. Chen was a thin man in late middle age, always precise, always immaculate, his authority so polished it almost disappeared into courtesy.

"The cups are a fraction too far apart."

Sonia looked and saw that he was right.

"I'm sorry, Sir."

"Do not be sorry. Be exact."

"Yes, Sir."

He watched her reset them.

"Better," he said. "Dr. Cheng values harmony. Harmony is not a slogan in this house. It is visible in every object and every person who serves here."

"Yes, Sir."

He inclined his head and moved on.

It should have been nothing. A correction. A sentence about household standards. But the words stayed with her because they named the thing she felt everywhere around her: not chaos, not panic, not even open cruelty, but a cultivated alignment in which every woman seemed arranged for someone else's ease.

Mrs. Huang was less polished and more dangerous for it.

When Sonia missed a streak on the mirrored leg of a side table, the housekeeper did not raise her voice. She only stood over her and made her do the entire room again.

"If you need praise for basic competence," she said, "you are in the wrong house."

Sonia swallowed her resentment and curtsied.

"Yes, Madam."

The dress rustled softly as she bent. The corset pressed at her ribs. She could feel sweat collecting at the base of her spine under the braces while she worked back through the room she had already done once. By the second pass she hated Mrs. Huang. By the end of the third, when the woman finally said, "acceptable," Sonia felt the ugly little pulse of relief approval always gave her now.

That was the most dangerous part.

Not the discipline.

The way her body answered it.

Days passed. The house opened itself to her in fragments: servant corridors hidden behind carved panels, dumbwaiters, polished staircases for guests and narrow service stairs for women carrying trays, the quiet wing where Cheng took private meetings, the laboratory suite accessible only to trusted staff and technical personnel. Sonia learned where not to look, which doors mattered, which guards nodded to house servants and which ones checked credentials every time.

What she learned fastest was that the house did not think of itself as cruel.

It thought of itself as correct.

Her first real look at Cheng came because Mrs. Huang sent her to fetch a briefcase from his private rooms and bring it to the conference room without delay.

"Do not dally," she said. "And do not look at anything that does not concern you."

"Yes, Madam."

His bedroom was larger than Sonia's entire childhood apartment. Dark wood, restrained luxury, no clutter except the clutter of a man important enough to leave work inside his private rooms and expect others to manage the overflow discreetly. The briefcase sat half open on a side desk with papers tucked into one pocket and a tablet sleeping black in the center. Sonia took it, shut it carefully, and hurried back through the service passage, the weight awkward against her ribs.

By the time she reached the conference room she was short of breath.

She stopped just outside the hidden servant entrance, forced her shoulders down, smoothed the front of her apron, and went in.

Cheng barely glanced at her when she placed the case beside him.

He was younger than she had expected from the files. Not young, but not old either. Elegant without softness. He wore authority the way some men wore good tailoring, as if it had been measured for him and improved his posture. Around the table sat engineers, administrators, and two men in government dress. A wall screen glowed with models Sonia could not read from where she stood.

"Leave it there," Cheng said.

"Yes, Sir."

She retreated to the wall.

He went on speaking as if she were furniture.

"People keep using the language of restriction," he said, tapping one finger lightly against the table. "Restriction is how undisciplined cultures describe structure when structure is first imposed. Later they stop experiencing it as loss. They experience it as intelligibility."

One of the engineers smiled as if she had heard this before and still enjoyed it.

Cheng looked at the screen.

"A society cannot remain stable while every appetite is treated as sovereign. Freedom without form produces noise. Anxiety. Waste. Isolation. We have corrected that."

A man in government dress said, "The European response remains uneven."

"Temporary," Cheng said. "Women adapt first in the body, then in habit, then in memory. Men adapt first in duty. After that the institutions follow. The body teaches the lesson, and society discovers the morality that matches it."

Sonia stood with her hands folded and kept her face empty while nausea turned slowly under her corset.

This was what frightened her more than shouting ideologues or obvious monsters ever could have.

He sounded reasonable.

He sounded proud of making domination feel like relief.

Another engineer brought up a rendering Sonia only half saw from the side: a smooth curve over the lower face, elegant enough to look almost decorative.

Cheng's attention sharpened.

"Good," he said. "Not punitive. Aspirational. It must feel like refinement before it feels inevitable."

Sonia did not let herself look too long.

Mrs. Huang's instructions had not included standing there listening, and the room was not safe. She curtsied the smallest amount necessary and left at the next pause in conversation, Cheng already speaking again behind her about rollout, affordability, and acceptability curves as if he were discussing transit design.

That night she lay awake in her attic room listening to the house settle around her and understanding that she could not leave without something real.

Suspicion was nothing.

She needed proof.

The chance came three days later.

A senior technician spilled solvent in one of the smaller lab spaces and the floor had to be cleaned before Cheng returned from an off-site meeting. Mrs. Huang chose Sonia because she had already been trusted near the private wing and because Sonia had learned to move quickly without looking hurried.

"Do the floor, the lower surfaces, and the sink area," Mrs. Huang said. "Nothing else. If anyone enters, you stop and make yourself useful."

"Yes, Madam."

The air in the lab carried the smell of alcohol and metal. White counters. Locked cabinets. Diagnostic equipment. Sonia wheeled in her cart, gloves already on, and felt her pulse begin to climb.

She cleaned what she had been told to clean first.

That mattered.

The camera in the corner would show a maid at work, not a thief lunging for secrets the second she crossed the threshold.

Only when the obvious tasks were done did she let herself drift closer to the side station where portable drives sat in a docking tray beside a terminal gone dark. Two USB sticks. One encrypted tablet. A paper folder clipped shut and marked with project initials she recognized from Mara's notes.

Her mouth went dry.

She kept moving.

Wipe the counter. Adjust the bottles. Lift the folder just enough to dust under it. Set it down. Slide one of the drives into the pocket hidden beneath the folded cleaning cloth. Do not rush. The air in the room felt painfully cold against the sweat gathering under her collar.

A sound in the corridor stopped her heart.

Voices. Male. Coming closer.

Sonia bent instantly over the sink and wrung out the cloth as the door opened.

Cheng entered with two researchers behind him.

He spared her one glance.

"Almost done?"

"Yes, Sir. This maid is finishing the sink area."

"Good."

He was already talking to the others before the word left his mouth.

"The behavioral layer is holding more cleanly in female cohorts. The reward loops are still too blunt in some male populations. I want elegance, not crudity."

Sonia kept her head down and cleaned the same stretch of steel twice while her heart slammed against the corset. The hidden drive felt enormous. Impossible. She could feel it even though it barely changed the weight of the cloth in her hand.

One of the researchers said, "And the suppression protocols?"

"Speech limitation is socially delicate," Cheng replied. "Visible coercion creates resistance. Voluntary adoption is better. Give them beauty, health logic, hygiene logic, status logic. Let silence arrive dressed as sophistication."

Sonia had to concentrate on her breathing to keep from showing anything on her face.

After another minute Cheng dismissed her with nothing more than, "Out."

"Yes, Sir."

She curtsied, rolled the cart away, and did not let herself walk faster until the service door had shut behind her.

In the laundry room she transferred the drive from the cloth pocket into the lining of her apron with hands that only shook a little.

Back in her room she locked the door, sat on the bed, and finally let herself breathe hard.

The drive held more than she had hoped for. Genomic annotations. Behavioral layering notes. Sex-differentiated compulsions. Memory editing references. Internal strategy language far uglier than the public rhetoric around harmony and social responsibility. Sonia copied everything to encrypted storage on her phone, uploaded a secured archive, then copied it again because fear made repetition feel like control.

When she finished, her fingers were stiff and the room felt too small to hold what she knew.

She did not sleep much.

By morning the plan had become simple.

Leave before the theft was noticed.

She went to Mrs. Huang just after breakfast service, when the housekeeper was reviewing inventory sheets and least likely to enjoy interruption.

Sonia stopped at the proper distance and folded her hands.

"Madam, if I may request a word."

Mrs. Huang did not look up immediately.

"Speak."

"My mother is gravely ill. I have received word this morning. I humbly ask permission to attend her."

Mrs. Huang's head lifted.

"Your duties are here."

"Yes, Madam. I understand."

"Then why are you asking?"

Because I have stolen from the man who signs your wages and I need to be on a plane before someone checks the lab access logs.

Sonia kept her expression soft and stricken.

"Because she is my mother, Madam."

Mrs. Huang's mouth tightened.

"Sentiment does not keep a house running."

"No, Madam."

It might have ended there if Mr. Chen had not entered midway through the exchange.

He took in the scene with one quick glance.

"A problem?"

Mrs. Huang answered before Sonia could.

"A request for leave at an inconvenient moment."

Mr. Chen looked at Sonia.

"Miss Sonia?"

She lowered her gaze further.

"Sir, my mother is very ill. I ask permission to go to her for a short time, if the household can spare me."

He was quiet for long enough to feel deliberate.

"Family obligations are not trivial," he said at last.

Mrs. Huang's face barely changed, but Sonia saw the displeasure there.

"Nor is household continuity," the housekeeper replied.

"Of course," Mr. Chen said mildly. "But Dr. Cheng's principles do not set family against duty. They organize duty properly."

He turned back to Sonia.

"You may go attend your mother. You will carry yourself as a representative of this household while you do so. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Sir."

"You will be discreet. You will remain orderly. You will return if return is possible."

"Yes, Sir."

It was the most dangerous moment of the entire theft, stranger even than the lab. He was being kind to her. Not soft, not equal, but sincerely kind inside the terms of the world that had made him. That sincerity made the lie uglier.

She curtsied.

"Thank you, Sir. Thank you, Madam."

Mrs. Huang dismissed her with obvious reluctance.

"Pack quickly. Leave your key."

"Yes, Madam."

Sonia did exactly that. She packed the few things she had brought, removed every trace of herself that was not already false, left the key where instructed, and went out through the servant entrance with one modest bag in her hand and the drive hidden close against her body.

Only when the taxi pulled away did she let herself look back.

The villa stood calm behind its gates, beautiful and controlled, the sort of place that made its own philosophy look inevitable.

At the airport she expected only relief.

Instead she found the next stage waiting for her under soft lights and polished signage.

The display stood just beyond security retail, branded with the quiet assurance of Mirende Ginciao. Two female mannequins faced the concourse in fitted coats and gloves. The first wore a half-mask in pale cream, shaped neatly over the mouth and nose and held by elegant side fittings that made it look almost medical until the eye adjusted and saw the intention beneath the styling. The placard called the line Jinshuwan. Hygienic. Refined. Public-minded.

The second mannequin wore something worse.

White porcelain glaze. Smooth cheeks. Only the eyes left open. The lower face sealed behind a polished shell so beautiful it took Sonia a second to register it as captivity. Beside it, under glass, lay the internal mouthpiece that anchored the thing in place.

Jurenmuse, the placard said.

Luxury silence.

Sonia stood still in the flow of passengers and felt cold move through her body from throat to stomach.

This was why Cheng had cared about elegance in the conference room. Why he had spoken about aspiration instead of punishment. The mask did not announce itself as violence. It announced itself as refinement, health, composure, social grace. Something a proper woman might choose because choice had already been bent into the shape they wanted.

An attendant beside the display adjusted a brochure and smiled at two women who stopped to look.

"The lighter line is ideal for daily wear," she said. "Very comfortable. Very modern."

One of the women touched the sample with gloved fingers.

"And the porcelain?"

"For formal use," the attendant said. "For women who value complete presentation."

Sonia moved away before they could see her expression.

She had spent days in a house where women were made invisible through service, posture, and obedience. Now she was looking at the next turn of the screw. Not only disciplined women. Silent women. Women rendered beautiful, useful, and unreadable from the mouth down.

The knowledge sat in her phone like heat.

By the time she reached her gate, relief had curdled into urgency.

She was not carrying home a theory anymore.

She was carrying evidence, a glimpse of intention, and the sick understanding that the world ahead would be gentler in language and harsher in fact than she had feared.

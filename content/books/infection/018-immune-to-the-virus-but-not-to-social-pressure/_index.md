# Immune to the Virus, but not to Social Pressure

During Sonia's absence, Geneva's Changed society had shifted even more.
Those who embraced their new mindsets found creative ways
to adapt their work and lives to the constraints of their altered behaviors.
Sonia noticed that dress codes had grown even stricter; proper attire had become crucial.
The dress code she had to follow in Shanghai was now common in Geneva too,
making her feel out of place without her corset and Gowbao Yoohoo.

As Sonia stepped onto the tram, she wove through the crowd,
her eyes drawn to the Changed women scattered throughout the car.
Dressed in their perfectly tailored outfits, they moved with an almost choreographed grace,
their corsets, gowns, and accessories reflecting a beauty that was both intimidating and captivating.

Sonia, hoping for a comfortable day and dressed without a corset and Gowbao Yoohoo, immediately felt out of place.
She found a vacant seat and relished the rare comfort of her less restrictive clothing.
But her relief was short-lived.
She quickly became the focus of a group of Changed women seated across from her.
Their gazes were sharp, their whispers barely concealed, as they noted Sonia's casual attire.

"Can you believe how she's dressed?" one woman murmured to another, loud enough for Sonia to hear.
"It's almost like she doesn't care about upholding standards of decency."

"Truly, it's a shame," another added, her tone full of disdain and pity.
"Her family must be mortified by such a display."

Their words, though spoken among themselves, were clearly aimed at Sonia.
They reminded her of the strict societal expectations now in place.
The scrutiny made Sonia shrink back in her seat, the weight of their judgment pressing down on her.

Sonia tried to lose herself in her book, but the isolation and judgment were overwhelming.
Each whisper felt like a nudge, pushing her back towards the standards she had only recently escaped.
"They see me as sloppy, a disgrace.
Is this really what my world has come to?" she wondered silently.

One of the women, unable to hold back her disapproval, spoke directly to Sonia.
"Dear, wouldn't you be more comfortable dressing properly?
It really does make a difference in how we present ourselves—and how we're perceived."

Her companion nodded, adding, "It's about respect, dear.
Not just for others, but for yourself and your family.
You'd do well to remember that."

The words stung, and Sonia felt a flush of embarrassment mixed with anger.
Her instinct was to retort, to defend her clothing choices and her right to comfort.
But she knew any response would likely escalate the tension.
Instead, she forced a tight smile and murmured, "Thank you for your concern."

Returning her gaze to her book, Sonia tried to focus, but the words blurred before her eyes.
The conversation around her underscored a change she was still struggling to accept.
"Am I really the one out of step here?" she questioned internally.

Even interactions with Anna became strained when Sonia did not conform.
At the Lakeside Café, she noticed the subtle reactions from Changed patrons to her casual attire.
Anna, epitomizing Changed elegance, suggested that Sonia might feel more at ease if she adhered to the new norms.
She praised Sonia's potential to excel within Changed society, should she choose to embrace their ways.
Sonia felt the pressure tighten around her like a vice,
a stark reminder of the widening gap between her and her surroundings.
She responded with a smile, using humor to deflect,
but inside, a storm of emotions raged—grief, frustration, and a stubborn spark of rebellion.

The next day, Sonia finally gave in to the pressure,
digging through her belongings to find the corset and Gobao Yoohoo blouse she had acquired in Shanghai.
She dressed with a sense of resignation, feeling the familiar constriction as the corset tightly embraced her torso.
The fabric of her dress flowed over the rigid structure,
subtly swaying with each hindered step she took due to the tight underskirt.

As she boarded the morning tram, the difference in how she was perceived was immediate.
The confined movements, dictated by the corset's embrace, altered her posture and gait,
broadcasting a silent signal of compliance and decorum.
Men on the tram noticed the change;
their offers of seats were not just acts of courtesy
but also tacit acknowledgments of her conformity to the expected feminine role.

One gentleman in particular stood up as she approached, gesturing to his seat with a respectful nod.
"Please, take my seat, Miss," he said, his voice gentle yet firm,
as if granting permission rather than making a suggestion.

Sonia paused, feeling the weight of expectation in his gesture.
With practiced grace, she curtsied,
lowering her gaze in a display of gratitude and deference—a movement
that had become second nature during her undercover assignment.
"Thank you, sir," she murmured.

As she perched on the edge of the offered seat,
the rustle of her underskirt mingling with the soft creak of the corset,
Sonia was acutely aware of the eyes upon her.
The act of sitting down, once thoughtless and routine, was now a calculated maneuver.
The corset enforced an upright posture,
and the Yoyah Sheengzoh underskirt demanded that her legs remain closely pressed together,
each aspect of her attire reinforcing her submission to the prevailing norms.

The ease with which she executed these movements startled her;
they had become ingrained, automatic responses,
echoes of her weeks as a housemaid in Shanghai.
The familiarity of her actions on the tram left her internally conflicted.
While the external acknowledgment of her compliance brought a strange sense of belonging and acceptance,
it also stirred a deep unease.
She was playing a role that was expected of her, yet it felt increasingly like a betrayal of her true self.

As she rode toward her destination, Sonia's mind was a tumult of emotions.
The simple acts of accepting a seat and curtsying had highlighted
how deeply the behaviors instilled in her during her time in Shanghai had taken root.
She was shaken not just by how others saw her
but by how naturally she had slipped into the guise of the Changed woman they expected to see.

Arriving at the Lakeside Café, Sonia noticed more women wearing masks.
She watched as Changed women entered the newly installed privacy cabins, likely to unmask and enjoy a moment of respite.
The sight of the masks still unsettled her—the idea of being gagged and silenced was a line she wasn't ready to cross.

Yet, as more women around her communicated fluently in hand-dancing,
laughing and gesturing with a grace she found both beautiful and alien, Sonia felt like an outsider.
She understood every gesture, but she refrained from participating, her hands resting uneasily in her lap.
Each movement they made was graceful, their laughter light and genuine,
communicated through the intricate ballet of hand-dancing.
Sonia felt a pang of longing, a desire to share in their happiness.

"Why do they seem so content?"
Sonia mused, watching a group of masked women re-enter the privacy cabins.
"Is it really just the virus, or is there something more?
Something I'm missing?"
Her hands twitched in her lap, mimicking the hand gestures she had learned but not used since her return.

"For so long, I've seen submission as weakness, as something imposed and unjust.
But these women… they don't seem oppressed.
They're not lesser; they're just different."
Sonia's heart ached with a mix of envy and confusion.
"Could it be that there's strength in their serenity?
Could embracing some of what they have actually bring me the peace I've been searching for?"

Her upbringing clashed with these thoughts.
The ideals of independence and outspokenness
that had been instilled in her now seemed strangely restrictive.
"Am I being true to myself by resisting this, or am I just clinging to what I've always been told is right?"

Sonia took a deep breath, her chest tight against the corset she had chosen that morning to blend in,
yet now symbolized so much more.
"Maybe it's time to stop judging them.
Maybe it's time to understand that happiness doesn't have one form.
Perhaps, just perhaps, I could allow myself to explore this part of me...
the part that might actually want to fit in with them.
It doesn't mean I have to lose who I am...
Maybe it's just about finding a new way to be me."

Later that day, Sonia found herself in a crowded market,
the buzz of conversation around her almost entirely visual,
a ballet of hands weaving stories in the air.
She watched, feeling an invisible wall between her and the world.
A vendor, a Changed woman with expressive eyes, tried to communicate an offer using fluid gestures.
Sonia understood but hesitated to respond in kind, her hands stubbornly by her sides.
The vendor's smile faded slightly, impatience flashing in her eyes.
Sonia reluctantly lifted her hands, her movements unwilling, an awkward mimicry of the surrounding grace.
She felt a flush of embarrassment,
her heart racing as she realized her reliance on voice now marked her as other, as less.
The pressure to adapt,
to fold her voice away and let her hands do the talking, weighed heavily on her,
like giving up.

# Decoding The Virus

Anna sent for Sonia late in the evening with a message so brief it made her hurry.

> Come tonight. I have enough to show you.

By the time Sonia let herself into Anna's underground lab, Anna was already there in a dark skirt and blouse, obviously corseted, but sleeves rolled neatly to the forearms, mask hanging from one wrist because she was alone below ground and had apparently forgotten to put it back on after drinking. The sight of her bare face bent over a screen hit Sonia with a strange force. Scientist first. Changed woman second. No, not second. Both at once.

Anna looked up at once and smiled.

"You're here."

"You said you had something."

"I do." Anna pushed a strand of hair back behind her ear with the back of her wrist, distracted, bright-eyed, almost feverish with work. "More than something, I think."

The lab bench had been taken over by printouts, sequence maps, notes in Anna's small sharp handwriting, and three separate screens glowing with layered genetic data Sonia could not begin to parse at a glance. On one tray sat two untouched cups of coffee Anna had apparently poured hours earlier and forgotten.

Sonia crossed to the central workstation.

"Tell me."

Anna breathed in once, steadying herself not from nerves but from the effort of compressing too much thought into speech.

"The viral genome is not behaving like a normal genome at all. Not in proportion. Not in intent. Most of it is payload." She touched the nearest screen. "It carries just enough machinery to infect, cross the blood-brain barrier, survive, replicate, and distribute its instructions. After that it becomes something closer to compressed architecture."

Sonia looked from the screen to Anna.

"Architecture for what."

"For people."

The answer came without hesitation.

Anna clicked to the next view, where colored regions spread across the display in dense bands and nested annotations.

"This section here is broad-spectrum. It appears in everyone. Knowledge packets, or something very close to them. Language acquisition. Skill clusters. Habit scaffolding. I can't map every piece yet, but it explains too much too neatly to be anything else."

"Chinese."

"Chinese, yes. Showyou. Domestic skills in some subjects. Professional confidence in others. Fine motor adjustments. Social scripts." Anna gave a brief helpless little gesture with one hand. "It's staggeringly efficient."

Sonia heard the admiration and felt her jaw tighten.

"Anna."

Anna stopped.

"I know," she said quietly. "I'm not forgetting what it is. Let me finish."

That took some of the edge out of Sonia's anger. Not much. Enough.

Anna turned back to the screen.

"The next layer is where it becomes uglier. Memory integration. It doesn't simply install behavior. It edits context. It folds the new values into existing personal history and then supplies enough false continuity to make the result feel native."

She brought up another set of notes.

"That explains the rituals. The certainty. Why Changed people remember rules, traditions, etiquette, gender expectations, all of it, as if they had always grown up inside them. The virus isn't merely making them obey. It's giving the brain a past that makes obedience feel like recognition."

Sonia leaned both hands on the edge of the bench.

"So when Elena says women should be seen and not heard—"

"That may be an implanted maternal memory. Or an edited one. Or a genuine memory reweighted until it becomes foundational." Anna met her eyes. "The point is that the brain no longer experiences it as foreign."

That landed hard enough that Sonia had to look away for a moment.

The fluorescent light reflected faintly in the metal surface of the sink. Somewhere behind the walls a pump clicked on and off.

"And the compulsions?"

Anna nodded and went to the third display.

"Sex-differentiated."

She said it the way a mathematician might say inevitable.

"The virus carries common material for everyone, but the behavioral enforcement branches. Female pathways are shaped toward obedience, service, modesty, dependence, feminization, and reward-linked submission. Male pathways toward authority, protectiveness, management, supervision, responsibility."

"Patriarchy," Sonia said flatly.

Anna's mouth softened with something almost apologetic.

"Engineered patriarchy, yes."

"Good. I just wanted to hear you say it."

Anna did not flinch.

"It is. That doesn't make the design less remarkable."

There it was again. The split. The thing Sonia kept coming up against whenever Anna worked on the data too long: moral clarity in one room of her mind, aesthetic and scientific rapture in the next, both real, neither strong enough to cancel the other.

"You admire it."

Anna looked down at the printout in her hand as if the paper itself deserved honesty.

"In part."

"Christ."

"Sonia." Anna set the paper down carefully. "If someone built a cathedral over a mass grave, the grave would still be a horror. The architecture might still be extraordinary."

Sonia stared at her.

"That is an appalling comparison."

"Yes," Anna said. "It is. It's also the closest thing I have to the truth."

For a few seconds neither of them spoke.

The lab hummed. Anna's forgotten coffee sat cooling beside the keyboard. Sonia felt the weight of the city above them, all those women on trams and in cafés and offices and shops moving through structures they remembered as normal because something microscopic had gone into their brains and taught them how to welcome the cage.

"Can it be undone?" she asked at last.

Anna exhaled slowly.

"Not cleanly. Not yet. Maybe not fully."

Sonia's stomach dropped.

Anna lifted a hand before she could speak.

"But that isn't the same as impossible."

She tapped a highlighted band in the genome map.

"The payload is segmented. Not elegantly separated, but segmented. Whoever made it expected modular expression. Some clusters appear conditional. Some clearly interact with pre-existing physiology before activating. Some are layered over others in a way that suggests timing, thresholds, reinforcement."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning if we understand how the instructions are structured, we may be able to interfere with parts of the program without having to unravel the whole virus at once."

"Memory first?"

"Maybe. Or reward structures. Or sex-specific compulsion cascades. I don't know yet."

Anna reached for a second printout and handed it across. Sonia took it, though most of it meant nothing to her beyond the red circles and notes.

"This is the part that concerns you personally," Anna said.

Sonia looked up.

"Me."

"Your immunity isn't complete immunity. That's the important distinction." Anna came around to her side of the bench so they were looking at the same page. "You received the knowledge layer. The social patterning. Some of the physical response scaffolding. You understand too much too intuitively for anything else to be true."

Sonia thought of Shanghai. The maid training. The tram. The instant curtsy to the man who offered her a seat.

"I know."

"But the coercive part misfired." Anna pointed to a small region of notation. "The virus binds to a target protein before one of the female compulsion cascades fully activates. In you, it seems to have attached to a neighboring structure with enough similarity to begin the process and enough difference to stop it completing correctly."

"So I got the language of the cage without the lock."

Anna's eyes flickered.

"Something like that."

"And if it had hit the right target?"

Anna did not answer immediately.

"You would feel less divided."

The quietness of it was worse than if she had sounded triumphant.

Sonia set the printout down.

"Less divided."

"Yes."

"You mean I would want all this."

Anna folded her hands together, thinking before she spoke.

"I think you already want parts of it."

That went through Sonia like a slap because it was too close to something she had been trying not to name.

"Anna."

"You asked for honesty."

"Not psychoanalysis."

"It isn't psychoanalysis." Anna's voice stayed very gentle. "It's pattern recognition."

Sonia turned away and walked two steps toward the sink, then back again because there was nowhere useful to put the surge of anger. The room was too small for pacing and too bright for hiding.

"Fine," she said. "What do we actually do with this."

Anna, mercifully, shifted with her.

"We keep going. I refine the map. I identify the memory-editing structures more clearly. I isolate the female and male enforcement bands as far as I can. We test hypotheses against expression patterns, not just sequence predictions. If I can get enough time with the analyzer downstairs at GAIA without questions being asked, I can accelerate some of it."

"Can you."

"Probably."

"Probably is not my favorite word this month."

Anna smiled despite herself.

"Mine either."

Sonia looked back at the screens.

For the first time since Shanghai, the data in front of her felt like more than horror and theory. It was still horror. It was still theory. But it had edges now. Structure. Places where a hand might pry.

"One genetic sequence at a time," she said.

Anna's expression sharpened with interest.

"Yes."

"You think that's possible."

"I think it may be the only sane way to approach it."

Sonia let out a breath she had not realized she was holding.

"All right."

Anna picked up her cup, remembered only after lifting it that the coffee would be cold, and put it down again.

"All right," she echoed.

Then, more softly: "I'm glad you brought this to me."

That was the danger too. Anna meant it in every register at once. As a scientist. As a friend. As a Changed woman to whom service had become an avenue toward joy.

Sonia looked at her and felt the same complicated gratitude and grief she always felt now.

"So am I," she said.

It was true.

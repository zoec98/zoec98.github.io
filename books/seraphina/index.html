                                                                                                                                                    In Victorian London and India, Seraphina endures punishment, widowhood, and scandal only to turn her enemies’ weapons into her own. Once a captive of corsets, boots, and veils, she rises to power as headmistress of Loch Thorne, reshaping the notorious reformatory into her vision of discipline and dignity. Yet with every corset laced tighter, every girl corrected into silence, she risks becoming the very figure she once sought to destroy. A darkly elegant story of revenge, control, and the uneasy boundary between survival and corruption.

                                                                                                                                                    India#

                                                                                                                                                    The humid air clung to Seraphina’s skin as she sprinted through the tangled jungle, the laughter of her childhood companions ringing in her ears. Her bare feet darted over roots and stones, her skirt hitched up in one hand, the other clutching a carved wooden toy—a gift from Raji, the boy who had taught her to climb trees and bait fishing lines. She lived for days like this: free, untamed, and blissfully ignorant of the strictures of British society her parents clung to like lifelines in this strange, hot land.

                                                                                                                                                    Her mother, Isabella, had tried once or twice to tame her. “A young lady must be presentable, Seraphina,” she’d said while pinning her unruly curls, only to have the child tear them loose moments later. Sir Alfred Lockewood, always impeccably dressed even in the oppressive heat, had been more lenient. “Let her run,” he’d said with a chuckle. “She’s happier out there than trapped under parasols and propriety.”

                                                                                                                                                    But happiness had a way of turning brittle under the weight of reality.

                                                                                                                                                    The day her parents died began like any other, with the rising sun painting the bungalow in shades of gold and red. Seraphina had been out in the clearing behind their home, showing Raji how to build a sling from scraps of leather. The sudden, frantic shouts of the servants brought them both to their feet.

                                                                                                                                                    “Miss! Miss Seraphina, come quickly!”

                                                                                                                                                    She ran toward the house, Raji on her heels, her chest tightening at the sight of smoke curling into the air. The front gate stood ajar, and the familiar grounds of the compound buzzed with chaos. British officers shouted commands as native soldiers swarmed the area, rifles at the ready.

                                                                                                                                                    Inside, the chaos was worse. Isabella’s voice, high-pitched and trembling, rang out from the sitting room. “Alfred, we must go! We must—”

                                                                                                                                                    “Stay here!” Sir Alfred barked, his pistol drawn. He turned to meet Seraphina as she burst through the doorway. “You!” he snapped, his usual warmth replaced with a tone she didn’t recognize. “Get upstairs and lock the door.”

                                                                                                                                                    “But—”

                                                                                                                                                    “Do as I say, Seraphina!”

                                                                                                                                                    The sharpness of his command froze her in place. She stumbled back toward the staircase, her heart pounding in her ears. At the top, she stopped, unable to resist peeking down as her father stepped outside, firing shots into the approaching mob.

                                                                                                                                                    Then she saw it: the flash of steel, the dark spray of blood as he staggered back into the doorway. Her mother screamed, rushing to his side, only to be dragged away by rough hands. Seraphina’s throat tightened as the scene blurred with tears. Her father fell, and her mother’s cry was abruptly silenced.

                                                                                                                                                    She didn’t remember fleeing the house. Someone must have pulled her away—Raji, perhaps, or one of the servants loyal enough to risk their lives. By the time she came to her senses, she was deep in the jungle, crouched in a thicket with a bloodied knife clenched in her trembling hand.

                                                                                                                                                    For weeks, she hid among the trees, surviving on roots and berries, avoiding every sound that hinted at pursuit. When British troops finally found her, she was a shadow of the carefree girl who had once roamed these woods. Her parents were gone, the bungalow a smoldering ruin, and the world she’d known had turned to ash.

                                                                                                                                                    They sent her to London, where her aunt awaited. Lady Agatha Thistledown’s letters were polite but cold, the tone of a woman burdened with a responsibility she hadn’t sought. Seraphina, numb and hardened, had no illusions about what awaited her.

                                                                                                                                                    India had taken her parents, her home, and her innocence. London would demand the rest. But she would not surrender easily.

                                                                                                                                                    London#

                                                                                                                                                    The drawing room’s air, thick with lavender and powdered propriety, vibrated with the sound of fabric tearing. Lady Agatha Thistledown—a name Seraphina thought ridiculously pompous even before arriving in London—clutched her hands together so tightly her knuckles whitened. She stared, horrified, as her niece hacked at the whalebone corset holding her with a wickedly curved knife she’d pulled from who-knew-where.

                                                                                                                                                    “Seraphina Arabella Lockewood!” Lady Thistledown’s voice was shrill, trembling between indignation and disbelief. “Cease this barbarism at once!”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina, crouched in the middle of the room like a tiger poised to pounce, glared at her aunt. Her dark hair tumbled wildly over her shoulders, the carefully pinned curls undone within moments of her arrival. She jabbed the knife into another taut ribbon, the blade gleaming under the chandelier. The corset snapped and fell from her body.

                                                                                                                                                    “Barbarism?” she snarled, her voice cutting through the genteel air like the knife through silk. “This blasted thing is barbaric. You’ve got to be bloody insane to wear it willingly.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown gasped, her hand flying to her throat. “Language! Such filth—I won’t allow it under my roof!”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina snorted and muttered something in Hindi—likely a curse, though her aunt wouldn’t have understood. Not that she cared. She grabbed the corset and hacked again at it with the blade, for good measure. It had been less than an hour since they’d forced her into the damned thing, and already her ribs ached and her temper blazed.

                                                                                                                                                    “This isn’t India!” Lady Thistledown stamped her foot, the most unladylike gesture Seraphina had yet seen her perform. “You will comport yourself as a lady, or you’ll be confined to your room until you learn proper decorum.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina paused, fixing her aunt with a glare sharp enough to pierce armor. “Confined?” she repeated, her tone dangerously low. “Do you think I’m some cow to be penned up? Try it, Aunt Agatha. Just try.”

                                                                                                                                                    The older woman faltered, retreating a step. “You—you are behaving like a savage!” she stammered. “And that blade—how dare you bring such a thing into my home? It’s—it’s indecent!”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina grinned—a flash of teeth that sent a shiver through her aunt. “Indecent, is it?” she said, flipping the knife expertly in her hand. “This knife has gutted fish and frightened off panthers. I’ll take indecent over suffocating, thanks.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Enough!” Lady Thistledown’s composure snapped, her voice rising above her usual measured hauteur. “You will stop this madness, or—”

                                                                                                                                                    “Or what?” Seraphina straightened, her torn corset dangling from her hand. The soft fabric of her chemise clung to her damp skin, her breath heaving in defiance. “You’ll marry me off to some stuffy lord with too much coin and not enough spine? Put me in another cage? I’d rather rot.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown’s lips pressed into a thin line, her face pale but for two spots of furious crimson on her cheeks. “You will be a lady,” she said, trembling with rage. “Even if it kills you.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Good luck with that,” Seraphina said, tossing the mutilated corset to the floor. The knife disappeared somewhere in her skirts with a fluid motion that spoke of years of practice. She crossed her arms, her chin high, daring her aunt to make another move.

                                                                                                                                                    The room fell silent except for the faint rustling of fabric as Lady Thistledown wrung her hands. The older woman’s mind raced—how to tame this wild creature thrust upon her care? Proper decorum had never faced a foe like Seraphina Arabella Lockewood.

                                                                                                                                                    Later on the same day, Seraphine spent her time outside in god knew what places. Meanwhile, the sitting room, with its pale green walls and collection of porcelain dogs, seemed an oasis of calm compared to the storm Seraphina had unleashed mere hours ago. Lady Thistledown sat stiff-backed on a settee, her teacup rattling slightly on its saucer. Across from her, Lady Prudence Ditheridge, a woman whose penchant for whispered scandals and practical solutions made her an invaluable confidante, leaned forward with an air of conspiratorial glee.

                                                                                                                                                    “My dear Agatha,” Lady Ditheridge began, her voice low and syrupy, “I could scarcely believe it when I heard of the girl’s… display.” She paused for emphasis, delicately brushing an invisible speck of dust from her sleeve. “But fear not. There is a solution, though it may require a touch of… fortitude.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown sighed, pressing a lace-edged handkerchief to her temples. “Fortitude? At this rate, I’ll require a saint’s patience and an iron will. You didn’t see her, Prudence—cutting at her corset like a madwoman! And the language. She speaks like a dockworker’s apprentice.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Indeed.” Lady Ditheridge nodded sagely, the small feather on her hat bobbing with each movement. “And this knife of hers—most unsettling. But do not despair. I have precisely the thing for such unruly behavior. Have you heard of The School for the Refinement of Wayward Ladies?”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown raised a brow. “No, but the name alone promises miracles.”

                                                                                                                                                    “It’s tucked away on a remote island in the middle of Loch Thorne, up in the Scottish Highlands,” Lady Ditheridge explained, her voice dropping to a hushed tone. “Practically unreachable, save for a single ferry that runs once on demand. It’s run by the formidable Miss Ethelred Blackthorn—an iron-willed woman of unparalleled discipline.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Blackthorn, you say? If names mean anything…”

                                                                                                                                                    “She’s aptly named,” Lady Ditheridge continued, her eyes alight with morbid enthusiasm. “Miss Blackthorn takes on only the most… challenging cases. She has a reputation for turning hoydens, rebels, and other such undesirables into paragons of grace and obedience. Her methods are, shall we say, firm.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown shuddered delicately. “What exactly do you mean by firm?”

                                                                                                                                                    “I mean precisely what you think, Agatha,” Lady Ditheridge said with a knowing smirk. “Corset training so rigorous the girls weep, etiquette drills until dawn, and strict behavioral correction. But the results! By the time they emerge, they’re the toast of society—marriageable, polished, and with figures as enviable as their manners.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown’s expression softened into cautious hope. “Marriageable, you say?”

                                                                                                                                                    “Oh, indeed. Graduates of Loch Thorne are snatched up faster than tickets to the opera. Wealthy families send their most hopeless daughters there, knowing they’ll return as the embodiment of refinement. Why, just last season, the Earl of Weatherby married a Loch Thorne alumna. And what a transformation she’d undergone—before her schooling, she was rumored to have boxed her brother’s ears in public!”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown nearly dropped her teacup. “And Miss Blackthorn succeeded with her?”

                                                                                                                                                    “Completely. She’s now the perfect countess, demure and obedient. Miss Blackthorn never fails.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown let out a long breath, her earlier despair giving way to cautious optimism. “Perhaps this is the answer. Seraphina must learn her place in the world, and if this school can accomplish what you claim…”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Ditheridge nodded, leaning in conspiratorially. “I’ll write to Miss Blackthorn on your behalf. Be prepared, though. Once Seraphina is there, the process will not be… gentle.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown clasped her hands tightly in her lap, the image of Seraphina’s defiant face still fresh in her mind. “Gentle or not, Prudence, I’ll do whatever it takes. That girl will not ruin herself—or our family’s name.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Ditheridge smiled with satisfaction. “Then it’s settled. By the time Miss Blackthorn has finished with her, Seraphina will be the toast of London society—or perish in the attempt.”

                                                                                                                                                    The three weeks had been nothing short of a siege, Lady Thistledown thought grimly as she ascended the staircase with her retinue of footmen. The carpeted steps muffled their march, but the tension was palpable. She gripped the folded letter from Miss Blackthorn like a lifeline, the crisp paper promising salvation from the chaos that had engulfed her once-orderly home.

                                                                                                                                                    The first disaster had been the curtains. Seraphina, for reasons only she could fathom, had decided to dismantle the heavy velvet drapes in the drawing room to create what she called “a proper jungle canopy.” The resulting mess—a tangled web of fabric, fallen curtain rods, and shattered porcelain vases—had sent Lady Thistledown’s maid into a fit of tears and the butler into a rage.

                                                                                                                                                    Then came the incident with the garden. Seraphina had enlisted the stable boy to help her dig a series of “trenches” in the flower beds, declaring it an experiment in irrigation she’d learned from her childhood companions in India. The roses were ruined, the lawn gouged with muddy channels, and the gardener had nearly quit on the spot.

                                                                                                                                                    But the final straw—the event that sealed her fate—had been the dining room. Seraphina had stormed in during a luncheon with Lady Ditheridge and several other esteemed guests, wearing trousers she’d fashioned from an old set of curtains, her hair wild and unpinned. She’d sat at the table and plunked a mango on the fine china. This clearly meant she had been breaking into Lady Ditheridges hothouse, and helped herself. Seraphina proceeded to cut it open with one of her ever-present knives, the juice dripping onto the pristine white tablecloth.

                                                                                                                                                    “You are a menace!” Lady Thistledown had shouted then, slamming her hand on the table and startling her guests.

                                                                                                                                                    “Better a menace than a corpse in a corset,” Seraphina had shot back, unfazed, as she bit into the fruit.

                                                                                                                                                    Now, standing outside Seraphina’s bedroom door, Lady Thistledown steeled herself. She nodded to the footmen, who burst in like soldiers storming a battlefield. Inside, Seraphina was perched on the edge of the bed, her knife already in hand.

                                                                                                                                                    “Oh, for heaven’s sake,” Lady Thistledown snapped, striding in. “Drop it, girl. You’ve no chance against four men.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina smirked, twirling the blade with practiced ease. “Took you long enough to come for me. Thought you might be enjoying the chaos.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Enjoying it? You’ve turned my home into a circus!” Lady Thistledown gestured to the footmen. “Take her things—pack everything. Leave nothing unchecked.”

                                                                                                                                                    As the footmen approached, Seraphina sprang to her feet, the knife flashing in her hand. “Touch my things, and you’ll regret it.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Oh, enough with the theatrics!” Lady Thistledown barked. “You’re going, whether you like it or not.”

                                                                                                                                                    Two of the footmen grabbed Seraphina’s arms while the others began ransacking the room. In her underwear, they found a blade sewn into the lining, its hilt cleverly hidden under a strip of lace. Another knife fell from a hidden pocket in her skirt, clattering to the floor.

                                                                                                                                                    “Is this really necessary?” Seraphina growled, struggling against the firm grip of the men. “You’re shipping me off like cargo.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Cargo doesn’t wield weapons,” Lady Thistledown said dryly. “And there’s more to find, I’m sure.”

                                                                                                                                                    When the trunk was opened, a footman pulled out a knife tucked between two slips, its edge glinting. Another emerged, hidden in the hollowed-out spine of a book. Two more were rolled into stockings, the fabric carefully stitched to keep the blades from falling loose.

                                                                                                                                                    By the time the search was complete, Seraphina stood bound at the wrists, glaring daggers—if not wielding them—at her captors. “You’re making a mistake,” she said, her voice low and venomous.

                                                                                                                                                    “The only mistake was thinking I could manage you myself,” Lady Thistledown retorted. “Now march.”

                                                                                                                                                    They hustled her down the stairs and out to the waiting carriage. Lady Thistledown, ever cautious, ordered a final inspection before boarding. A footman patted Seraphina down, discovering yet another knife strapped to her thigh with a strip of leather. The girl grinned wickedly as it was confiscated.

                                                                                                                                                    “You’re thorough, I’ll give you that,” Seraphina said as they shoved her into the carriage.

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown climbed in after her, ensuring the bonds were secure and the trunk locked tight. “If you attempt anything during this journey, I’ll see to it that Miss Blackthorn hears every sordid detail of your behavior.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina leaned back against the seat, her defiance undimmed. “You’re in for a long ride, Auntie.”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown settled into her seat, the letter from Miss Blackthorn still tucked safely in her pocket. “Not as long as you think, my dear. By the time we reach Loch Thorne, you’ll have no fight left in you.”

                                                                                                                                                    Loch Thorne#

Seraphina sat bound and gagged, her eyes burning with defiance even as the chill bit at her cheeks. Lady Thistledown, wrapped in a heavy wool cloak, clutched the edges of her seat with a grim determination. She avoided her niece’s gaze, focusing instead on the looming silhouette of the island ahead.

An arched wrought-iron gate stood at the end of the dock, the institution’s name, The School for the Refinement of Wayward Ladies, painted in austere black letters on a weathered white sign. The words felt heavy, like the very air of this forsaken place.

The driver, an expressionless man in a dark coat, tipped his hat as he took the reins. Lady Thistledown climbed in first, her skirts rustling against the leather seat. Ahead, the reformatory loomed. Built from Craigleith sandstone—the same cold, gray stone as Edinburgh’s ancient heart—it was an unforgiving structure, its three stories framed by two severe side wings. The building exuded an air of control, its blank windows like unblinking eyes. Behind it, smaller outbuildings for the kitchen and servants huddled in its shadow, as if cowed by its presence.

                                                                                                                                                    The carriage halted before the main doors. A maid stood waiting, her silhouette stark against the heavy wooden entryway. Her dress was severe, her waist cinched so tightly it was impossible to ignore the rigidity of her posture. She curtsied deeply as they approached, her expression void of warmth.

                                                                                                                                                    “Welcome to Loch Thorne,” she said softly, her voice clipped and precise. She turned to Lady Thistledown and added, “Miss Blackthorn is expecting you.”

                                                                                                                                                    The maid led them through the imposing doors into a marble-floored hall. The cold seeped into the very air, the scent of polish and discipline woven into every stone. Lady Thistledown walked briskly, but her glance at the tapestries lining the walls betrayed her unease. Seraphina followed under guard, her bound hands still held tight by the footmen.

The maid knocked twice before pushing it open to reveal Miss Blackthorn’s study. The room was severe yet meticulously organized: shelves lined with books on deportment and discipline, a polished desk free of clutter, and a single chair positioned precisely before it.

The governess was tall, her posture as unyielding as the stone walls surrounding them. Her dark hair was pulled into a flawless bun, a single streak of silver cutting through the midnight strands like a blade. Her dress, high-collared and jet black, left no doubt about the strictness of her character. The corset beneath it was no mere garment; it was a declaration, cinched so tightly that her every movement seemed measured by the demands of perfect control. Her pale face bore not a flicker of humor, her sharp features carved into an expression of uncompromising authority.

                                                                                                                                                    “Lady Thistledown,” Miss Blackthorn greeted, her voice smooth and icy. She inclined her head with a precise formality, her dark eyes locking onto her guest. “You are most welcome at Loch Thorne.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Miss Blackthorn,” Lady Thistledown replied, her voice steadier than she felt. “I trust you have received my correspondence.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Indeed,” Miss Blackthorn said. “We are prepared for Miss Lockewood.” She gestured vaguely toward Seraphina, still bound and gagged. For all the attention she paid the girl, she might as well have been luggage.

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown hesitated, but Miss Blackthorn’s unwavering gaze silenced any second thoughts. “She is… a challenge,” Lady Thistledown admitted.

                                                                                                                                                    “Challenges are my specialty,” Miss Blackthorn said coolly. She turned her attention back to the maid. “Prepare Miss Lockewood for intake. Lady Thistledown, would you care for tea while we finalize the arrangements?”

                                                                                                                                                    Lady Thistledown nodded, relieved to hand over her burden. “Yes, thank you. That would be… most welcome.”

The maid led Seraphina, still handled by two footmen, through an icy hallway, the stone walls swallowing any warmth from the sparse lamplight. Broad-shouldered and solid, she stood like a monolith, her severe expression set into features as hard as the stone walls around them. Her dark hair was pulled into a functional braid, and her pale skin was marked by a faint scar tracing the edge of her jaw. We begin.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina managed a polite nod, though her mind churned with thoughts. The house was beautiful, no doubt, but it was another gilded cage, another stage on which she would play the obedient wife while she bided her time. She sat stiffly on the edge of a divan, her posture dictated by the corset, and gazed out at the garden beyond the French windows.

                                                                                                                                                    This was India, her homeland, a place she had longed for. But the freedom she had associated with it felt as distant as ever.

                                                                                                                                                    House in Madras#

                                                                                                                                                    The Major took Seraphina on an unexpected tour of the house and gardens, his demeanor notably softer than it had been during the voyage. He seemed proud of his home, his voice carrying a warmth and enthusiasm that contrasted sharply with his usual sternness.

                                                                                                                                                    The first remarkable space he led her to was the sunroom, an expansive, airy space lined with tall, arched windows that framed the lush garden outside. The tiled floor gleamed in soft cream and blue patterns, and wicker furniture with embroidered cushions was arranged in inviting clusters. Hanging ferns and pots of fragrant jasmine softened the room, their greenery spilling over polished brass stands. The sunlight filtering through the windows made the room glow, and an intricately carved marble fountain stood in the center, its gently trickling water filling the air with a soothing melody.

                                                                                                                                                    “This was my mother’s favorite place,” the Major said, his voice quieter as he gestured around. “She believed the light here was good for the soul.”

                                                                                                                                                    The second place was far more exotic–a private study, tucked away at the back of the house. The room was dimly lit, its walls lined with bookshelves that groaned under the weight of leather-bound volumes. A massive carved desk stood in the center, its surface cluttered with maps, papers, and curiosities from the Major’s travels. On one wall hung an ornate tapestry depicting a hunting scene, the figures rendered in vibrant reds and golds. But the centerpiece of the room was what the Major called a Himalayan prayer machine.

                                                                                                                                                    The contraption was made of dark, polished wood, its surface carved with intricate patterns that swirled like tendrils of smoke. At first, it appeared to be a closed cabinet, but with a practiced hand, the Major opened it like a Christian altar, revealing its dazzling interior. Several golden cylinders, encrusted with glittering gems, spun smoothly within, their surfaces engraved with swirling designs.

                                                                                                                                                    “I found it on a survey expedition near the Tibetan border. Odd little village. I doubt it’s on any official map.” He turned one of the cylinders, discreetly looking away from the device. “It is amazing. Watch,” he said.

                                                                                                                                                    The machine emitted a low, monotonous hum that reverberated through the room. The cylinders caught the sunlight streaming in from a nearby window and scattered it into a cascade of ever-changing, multicolored patterns. The light danced on the walls and ceiling, shifting and shimmering like a living mosaic. The machine’s hum rose and fell like distant chanting–or maybe it was just in her head.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina found herself transfixed. The hum of the prayer machine resonated in her chest, and the mesmerizing patterns seemed to pull her thoughts into their endless spiral. She didn’t realize how long she had been staring until she felt the Major’s fingers under her chin, tilting her face upward.

                                                                                                                                                    Her gaze snapped back to his, and she blinked, disoriented. His expression was softer than she had ever seen it, his blue eyes holding hers for a moment before he leaned down and kissed her gently on the lips. Her lips parted in surprise, not resistance–her thoughts still caught in the slow spiral of color. The Major’s kiss landed softly, but her awareness didn’t return until it had already passed. When he pulled back, he lingered, studying her face as though searching for a reaction.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina’s heart raced, but she kept her composure, her expression carefully neutral. She hadn’t expected this, not from the man who had treated her as little more than a doll to dress and display. For a moment, she wondered if the prayer machine had affected him as it had her–if its strange hum had opened a crack in his disciplined exterior.

                                                                                                                                                    The Major straightened, his usual demeanor returning as he closed the prayer machine with a quiet click. “It’s remarkable, isn’t it?” he said, his voice steady but still carrying a hint of something softer.

                                                                                                                                                    “Yes,” Seraphina replied, her voice calm despite the lingering warmth of his kiss. “It’s… captivating.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina’s days in Madras settled into a predictable, almost meditative rhythm. Mornings began with Amara dressing her in the Major’s prescribed attire: the reformatory-standard corset laced to perfection, stockings, gloves, heels, a full gown, and, if leaving the house, a cape and veil. Breakfast with the Major was quiet but cordial, the meal taken in the shaded dining room, where ceiling fans turned lazily above, stirring the warm, humid air.

                                                                                                                                                    If the Major worked from home, Seraphina accompanied him to his study, where he would inevitably open the prayer machine for her. The hum and glittering patterns quickly drew her into their spell, her thoughts dissolving into the kaleidoscope of light and sound. Only when the Major gently lifted her chin and kissed her–and he always did this when the machine had run its course–would she tear herself away, her thoughts both scattered and sharpened by the strange intimacy of the gesture.

                                                                                                                                                    The bustling markets around the Kapaleeshwarar Temple were a world apart from the serene confines of the Major’s estate. The temple itself stood majestically, its brightly colored gopuram towering over the scene, carved with intricate depictions of gods, goddesses, and mythical creatures. The air was thick with the scent of jasmine and incense, mingling with the tang of spices and the earthy aroma of fresh produce.

                                                                                                                                                    The market buzzed with life, vendors shouting to advertise their wares as people jostled for space in the narrow, winding lanes. Stalls overflowed with offerings: baskets of vibrant fruits–mangoes, guavas, bananas; mounds of turmeric, cumin, and saffron in golden and orange hues; silken saris in every imaginable color, their threads glinting in the sunlight; and rows of intricately carved wooden idols and brass lamps. Strings of fresh jasmine and marigolds hung from vendor awnings, their fragrance carried on the occasional breeze.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina, veiled and swathed in her formal cape, was a striking figure among the locals. Heads turned as she passed, her tall, corseted form and heavily layered attire standing out amidst the flowing, lightweight fabrics of the women around her. Amara walked at her side, cheerful and chatty, guiding her through the chaos with practiced ease.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina made it a point to linger at stalls that sold items of particular interest. She noted the availability of herbs, powders, and tinctures, making polite inquiries under the guise of seeking remedies for minor ailments. Her sharp eyes cataloged the layout of the market: Where certain goods were sold, which vendors seemed discreet, and where specialized items could be acquired.

                                                                                                                                                    Amara’s cheerful demeanor made her a natural negotiator, and Seraphina frequently allowed her maid to purchase small items for herself–an embroidered scarf, a packet of sweets–earning her favor. “You are most kind, Memsahib,” Amara would say, her smile wide as she tucked the purchases into her basket.

                                                                                                                                                    Returning to the house, Seraphina would retreat to the coolest room–a shaded space on the ground floor with marble floors that retained a faint chill. She shed her cape and veil, savoring the relative freedom from their oppressive weight, and settled onto a divan to read. The climate, while still stifling, was manageable here, the quiet punctuated only by the occasional chirp of birds or the soft rustle of a breeze through the garden outside.

                                                                                                                                                    Writing was a more frustrating endeavor. Her gloves, ever-present and stiffened, made handling a pen a clumsy task. Still, she persisted, the act of putting ink to paper a small rebellion against her restrictions. Her handwriting had adapted over time, the once-flowing script now precise and measured to accommodate her limitations.

                                                                                                                                                    The Major’s departure came with little fanfare, his routine reconnaissance mission taking him away for a week. Seraphina, watching him ride off from the shaded verandah, felt relief, and a little trepidation. The weight of his presence was momentarily lifted, leaving her freer than she had been since her arrival. Yet, the absence of his command left her unmoored, the boundaries she despised now disturbingly absent.

                                                                                                                                                    Seeking a semblance of control, she approached Amara and requested her help in designing a more traditional Indian lehenga choli outfit for herself. The Major had permitted her to do that, to her surprise. She envisioned flowing, vibrant silks and the loose elegance of traditional Indian attire. But when the time came to actually craft the outfit, something in her rebelled.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina began with her corset, her fingers tightening the laces with practiced ease. The thought of being uncorseted–of feeling the soft, uncontrolled expanse of her waist beneath the silk–sent a shiver of discomfort through her. The corset, laced to its usual reformatory standards, provided the familiar compression and rigid support she couldn’t seem to abandon. Over this she fitted the choli, its heavy Bengali silk vibrant with color and pattern. But rather than the soft, flowing fabric she had first imagined, she was compelled to make alterations that were anything but traditional.

                                                                                                                                                    The choli featured a high, stiffened collar, doubling as a neck stretcher. Reinforced boning hidden beneath the intricate embroidery ensured her head remained perfectly upright, her neck elongated in an elegant yet unyielding posture. The collar’s rigid edge was concealed beneath decorative beading, but its functional design was unmistakable as it held her head with regal precision.

                                                                                                                                                    The sleeves, long and fitted, extended to her wrists, where they buttoned tightly. More unsettlingly, she felt she had to insist on sewing the upper sleeves to the body of the blouse, restricting the movement of her arms from the elbows upward. The constriction felt… necessary, though she couldn’t articulate why. Beneath the blouse, she donned leather gloves that were boned at the wrist and so tightly fitted they felt like a second skin.

                                                                                                                                                    Her lehenga, a full-length skirt of luxurious silk, was fitted over layers of petticoats. The hem skimmed the ground, its weight requiring the lift provided by her standard five-inch heeled boots. Within the skirt, subtle panels tugged at her knees, forcing her legs to move in a restrained, graceful shuffle. Despite the discomfort, the restriction felt like an anchor against the chaotic unease that threatened to rise whenever she considered abandoning such limitations.

                                                                                                                                                    For her dupatta, she chose a semi-transparent fabric, fine but weighted at the edges with delicate beadwork that ensured it draped perfectly. It veiled her face just enough to obscure her vision, the world beyond its edge a softened blur. To keep it secure, she concealed a stiffened hood beneath the drape, wired and lightly boned to hold the dupatta in place regardless of movement.

                                                                                                                                                    Finally, she adorned herself with jewelry: a wide, choker-style necklace of gold and pearls that covered the seam where the choli’s neck collar met her skin, and heavy gold armlets at her wrists, their design both decorative and suggestive of restraint. Her boots, though hidden beneath the lehenga, were adorned with tiny ghungroo bells, their soft chimes accompanying her every step like a subtle announcement of her presence.

                                                                                                                                                    When she stood before the mirror, the sights both captivated and unsettled her. The outfit was stunning, a blend of Victorian restraint and Indian opulence, but its design betrayed her supposed yearning for freedom. The collar held her head high, the gloves and sleeves immobilized her arms, the skirt hobbled her steps, and the veil clouded her vision. She felt bound, restrained, confined.

                                                                                                                                                    Yet when she tried to imagine loosening these elements–removing the collar, freeing her arms, or wearing flat sandals–an inexplicable wave of panic welled within her, accompanied by a sense of wrongness so profound it made her hands tremble. It was as if her body and mind rejected the idea outright, the thought of unrestrained freedom too alien to endure.

                                                                                                                                                    Amara entered the room and gasped softly, her expression a mixture of admiration and surprise. “Memsahib, you look… divine. Like a queen.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina managed a smile, though her reflection filled her with unease. “Thank you, Amara,” she said softly, turning to take a step. The bells at her ankles chimed with every careful movement, each sound a reminder of her self-imposed captivity.

                                                                                                                                                    She didn’t understand what was happening to her. She yearned for freedom, yet every attempt to embrace it left her retreating into a cage of her own making. It wasn’t just the Major’s standards binding her anymore–it was something deeper, something she couldn’t name. As she moved through the house, her thoughts churned, the chime of her steps echoing like a question she had yet to answer.

                                                                                                                                                    The next morning, Seraphina awoke with the same pull she had felt every morning since arriving at the Major’s house. Even without his presence, her steps guided her to his study, the chime of her ghungroo bells echoing softly in the quiet halls. Dressed in her restrictive Indian ensemble, she moved carefully, each shuffle of her knees dictated by the panels sewn into her skirt, her arms barely able to lift in their confining sleeves.

                                                                                                                                                    With deliberate effort, she reached the Himalayan prayer machine, maneuvering with difficulty to open its carved wooden doors. The cylinders gleamed in the morning sunlight as her gloved hands turned them, their hum swelling to fill the room. The lights refracted through her veil, muted and kaleidoscopic, casting ever-shifting patterns onto the walls.

                                                                                                                                                    The effect was immediate, lulling her into a trance-like state. She sank onto a nearby chair, her posture rigid as always, and let her thoughts drift into the swirling light and sound. Time became meaningless. When she finally blinked back to awareness, the cylinders were still, the hum long gone. The room was quiet, the sunlight higher now, signaling the late morning hour. She glanced down and saw a dark stain on her blouse–a trace of spittle that had escaped her while she was lost in thought. A faint blush of embarrassment rose to her cheeks.

                                                                                                                                                    “Amara,” she called softly.

                                                                                                                                                    Her maid entered promptly, her sharp eyes catching the stain. “Memsahib, what happened?” she asked, concern flickering across her face.

                                                                                                                                                    “Nothing,” Seraphina replied with measured calm. “Please clean this for me.”

                                                                                                                                                    Amara dabbed at the spot with a cloth and murmured something about tea before leading Seraphina out to the markets. The heat outside was stifling, her layers of silk and boning insulating her like a furnace, but Seraphina hardly noticed. The thought of wearing anything looser, anything lighter, sent a ripple of unease through her. Improper. Indecent. The words and concepts echoed in her mind, unbidden but absolute.

                                                                                                                                                    The markets around Kapaleeshwarar Temple teemed with life as always. The vibrant stalls overflowed with fruits, spices, and fabrics, the air filled with the calls of vendors and the mingling scents of incense and jasmine. Seraphina, veiled and swathed in her elaborate outfit, stood out starkly among the locals, her every step accompanied by the soft chime of her bells.

                                                                                                                                                    It was here that Seraphina began to set the next stage of her plan into motion. Her meticulous trips to the market, her careful observations of the stalls and their offerings, had led her to a Vaidhyar, a traditional healer named Govindan, whose reputation for discretion and potent remedies was whispered among those in need of unconventional solutions.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina had learned of Govindan through a conversation overheard in the market weeks earlier. Two women, standing near a stall of spices, had spoken in hushed tones about a certain “Chaturanga Sudha,” a substance said to solve the insurmountable. With careful inquiries cloaked in curiosity about herbal remedies, Seraphina had pieced together enough information to find him.

                                                                                                                                                    This morning, as Amara bowed her head in prayer at the Kapaleeshwarar Temple, Seraphina excused herself to explore a nearby lane. Govindan’s shop was a humble, nondescript space tucked between stalls selling turmeric and brassware. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of dried herbs and burning camphor. Bundles of leaves hung from the ceiling, and rows of clay jars lined the walls, each labeled with neat Tamil script.

                                                                                                                                                    Govindan was a thin man with sharp eyes and a calm demeanor, his hands busy grinding something into powder as Seraphina entered. He looked up, his expression unreadable, and inclined his head.

                                                                                                                                                    “Memsahib,” he greeted, his voice low and steady. “How can I assist you?”

                                                                                                                                                    “I have heard you provide remedies,” Seraphina said, her voice soft but deliberate. “I seek… discretion.”

                                                                                                                                                    His eyes narrowed slightly, but he nodded. “You are looking for Chaturanga Sudha.”

                                                                                                                                                    She nodded. “Yes.”

                                                                                                                                                    Without further question, Govindan turned to a small locked cabinet and withdrew a tiny flask. The liquid inside was a rich amber-gold, its viscosity catching the dim light. He placed it on the counter, his movements precise and deliberate.

                                                                                                                                                    “This is Visha Prabha,” he said, his tone calm. “It is potent. Irrecoverable. Five days, no more, and the symptoms will mimic cholera. Use it wisely.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina reached into her purse and handed him a folded note of rupees. Govindan took it without a word and slipped the flask into a small silk pouch, which he handed to her. She tucked it into the folds of her dupatta, her gloved fingers brushing the cool fabric as she concealed it.

                                                                                                                                                    “Thank you,” she said, her tone measured.

                                                                                                                                                    Govindan inclined his head again, his face impassive. “May it bring you peace.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina returned to Amara by the temple steps, her expression serene despite the storm of thoughts swirling within her. As they walked back through the market, her bells chiming softly with each step, she felt the weight of the small pouch hidden beneath her veil.

                                                                                                                                                    The next stage of her plan had begun.

                                                                                                                                                    The day Seraphina applied the Visha Prabha to the Major’s chai was unremarkable in its routine. The house staff bustled about their tasks, Amara attended to her mistress with cheerful efficiency, and the Major himself sat at breakfast, reading correspondence and sipping his beloved spiced tea. Seraphina, poised and serene, ensured his cup was refilled. The amber liquid swirled as it poured, the faint scent of cardamom and cloves masking the toxin’s floral undertone.

                                                                                                                                                    By mid-afternoon, the first symptoms appeared. The Major complained of stomach cramps and nausea, dismissing them at first as the result of too much rich food the previous night. By evening, his condition had worsened. He grew pale and clammy, his brow damp with sweat as waves of vomiting and diarrhea wracked his body. The staff rushed to fetch Amara and Seraphina, who both appeared at his bedside, the picture of concern.

                                                                                                                                                    “Sebastian,” Seraphina said softly, her gloved hand resting lightly on his forehead, “you must rest. Amara, bring water, quickly.”

                                                                                                                                                    Amara returned with a pitcher and began dabbing at the Major’s face with a cool cloth. Seraphina, ever composed, insisted on personally preparing another cup of his favorite chai, her movements precise as she poured the tea and stirred it with care. When she brought it to him, her voice was soothing, almost tender. “Here, darling. This will help you feel better.”

                                                                                                                                                    The Major, too weak to argue, drank the tea with trembling hands. The act of caring for him, of gently coaxing him to sip the very poison she had prepared, filled Seraphina with a strange sense of calm. She attended to him diligently, her presence constant, her demeanor patient and loving.

                                                                                                                                                    The disease progressed rapidly. The Major’s once-strong body withered as dehydration set in, his skin losing its color and elasticity, his eyes sunken and glassy. Violent spasms gripped him, leaving him gasping for air between fits of retching. The smell of sickness permeated the room despite the efforts of the staff to keep it clean.

                                                                                                                                                    An English doctor arrived within two days, his face grim as he examined the Major. “Cholera,” he declared, his voice heavy. “We must do what we can to keep him hydrated, though it may already be too late.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina maintained her role perfectly, a devoted wife desperate to save her husband. She sat by his bedside, holding his hand in hers, her gloves immaculate despite the chaos. Amara worked tirelessly at her side, ensuring the linens were changed, the room aired, and the pitcher of water never ran dry. Seraphina continued to serve him chai, her voice soothing as she whispered reassurances he would never live to see fulfilled.

                                                                                                                                                    The Major succumbed within a week. His final hours were marked by unbearable pain, his body wracked with convulsions and fever. Seraphina sat with him until the end, her hand resting lightly on his as his breath grew shallow, then ceased altogether. When the doctor declared him gone, she closed her eyes for a moment, letting a single tear slide down her cheek.

                                                                                                                                                    Victorian mourning enveloped her almost immediately. The Major’s death necessitated strict observance of custom, and Seraphina found herself clad in heavy black gowns trimmed with crepe. A high, stiff collar replaced her neck corset, its unyielding structure a familiar comfort. The bodices were tightly fitted, and the skirts weighed her down with layers of somber fabric. A veil of fine black lace obscured her face, and gloves, now black instead of white, covered her hands.

                                                                                                                                                    Though the Major’s absence should have brought her freedom, Seraphina found herself unable to relinquish the restraints. Whenever she attempted to loosen her laces or remove a layer of mourning dress, a wave of unease swept over her, leaving her breathless and disoriented. She quickly abandoned the effort, resigning herself to the bindings that felt both necessary and inexplicably right.

                                                                                                                                                    In the mornings, she sought solace in the Himalayan prayer machine, her veiled figure seated before it like a penitent before an altar. With great effort, she opened the contraption, her stiff arms maneuvering the cylinders into motion. The hum filled the room, vibrating through her ribs, and the glittering patterns of light danced across the walls.

                                                                                                                                                    She stared at the shifting colors, her thoughts fragmenting and reforming in time with the mesmerizing display. The Major’s face flickered in her mind, then dissolved into a kaleidoscope of memories and plans. The hum soothed her, the light enveloped her, and for a few moments, the weight of mourning–of guilt, of resolve–seemed to lift.

                                                                                                                                                    When the cylinders stilled, and silence returned, Seraphina rose, her movements slow and deliberate. She adjusted the veil over her face, its soft lace brushing against her skin, and turned to leave the room. Though the Major was gone, the bindings remained, her mourning as much a part of her as the revenge she had carried out.

                                                                                                                                                    House in Madras#

                                                                                                                                                    The months following the Major’s death unfurled with the relentless formality of Victorian mourning customs. Seraphina found herself enveloped in layers of deep mourning attire, her figure now constantly swathed in heavy black fabrics well suited to the restrictive clothing she had grown accustomed to. Her corsets remained tightly laced, her posture impeccable, and her movements measured by the confines of her attire. A thick veil obscured her features, casting her world in a dim, muted light.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina was occupied with the responsibilities of organizing the Major’s burial and managing the affairs of the estate. The house in Madras, though a gilded cage, ran smoothly under the diligent attention of the household staff. Meanwhile, the legal process of obtaining probate for the Major’s assets commenced, both in England and India.

                                                                                                                                                    A trusted family solicitor, Mr. Edwin Hawthorne, handled the proceedings in England. He was a seasoned barrister known for his discretion and efficiency. In India, a local executor, Major Arthur Collins–an old comrade of the Major–managed the estate’s legal affairs. Both men were sympathetic to the plight of the young widow, struck so cruelly by fate so soon after her marriage. Their efforts ensured that the proceedings moved as quickly as the bureaucracy allowed, though the process still stretched over several months– especially the legendary Indian bureaucracy took its time to run its course.

                                                                                                                                                    Throughout this period, Seraphina found herself inexplicably drawn to the Himalayan prayer machine in the Major’s study. Its hum and lights captivated her, offering a strange sense of peace and support through these trying times. She developed a routine, visiting the study at least once a week, though more often every other day or every three days, as the pull of the machine grew stronger. When too much time passed without a session, she felt a restless unease, an increasing need to return. Though she couldn’t comprehend why she felt such a compulsion, the connection was undeniable. The Major, through the machine, exerted a hold on her that persisted beyond the grave.

                                                                                                                                                    The War Office provided a widow’s stipend, and the Major’s family—though distant—ensured she received a modest allowance. These sources of income allowed Seraphina to maintain a comfortable life without dipping into her inheritance, which she guarded with deliberate care. That capital was reserved for a singular purpose: to bring ruin to those who had twisted her into an obedient, veiled, and silent dress puppet— the Thistledowns, and Mrs. Blackthorn.

                                                                                                                                                    During this time, her days were filled with the formalities of mourning and the endless paperwork required to secure the Major’s assets. Once the grant of probate was finally obtained, she gained the legal authority to liquidate the estate in India. She discreetly sought a buyer for the Madras mansion, insisting on a fair price despite the murmurs among the local expatriate community.

                                                                                                                                                    The transaction took time, but it was eventually completed. The sale produced substantial funds, which she transferred to accounts under her sole control. With the house emptied, her luggage and remaining personal effects were moved to the Hotel d’Angelis—a respectable and well-appointed establishment catering to European travelers in Madras.

                                                                                                                                                    So the day came when Seraphina stood alone in the drawing room, draped in black. Her mourning gown swept silently across the polished floor as she faced the assembled household staff for the final time.

                                                                                                                                                    “You have all served with dedication,” she said, her voice calm and clear. “I am grateful. With the house sold, I must release you from your duties. Each of you will receive a letter of recommendation, and a final bonus as a token of thanks.”

                                                                                                                                                    There were murmurs of appreciation, nods of understanding. One by one, they departed—some with bowed heads, others with lingering glances.

                                                                                                                                                    Only Amara remained, standing quietly at the back, her gaze fixed on Seraphina.

                                                                                                                                                    “I want you to stay with me,” Seraphina said, turning to her. “I’ll need your help.” Amara inclined her head. “Of course, memsahib.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina attempted to shed her mourning garments, but the moment her fingers touched the corset strings, her hands began to tremble. The act of unlacing it left her breathless–not with relief, but panic. The thought of removing her veil or gloves filled her with an inexplicable dread.

                                                                                                                                                    Within minutes, she had dressed herself again, the tight fabric pressing in, restoring something that felt dangerously close to calm.

                                                                                                                                                    “Why do I feel like this?” she asked aloud, her voice hollow in the now silent house.

                                                                                                                                                    Amara, as ever, appeared soundlessly beside her. “Perhaps it is the prayer machine, memsahib. The light, the sound–it is not a natural thing.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina said nothing at first. Her thoughts drifted to the carved wooden cabinet in the study–the Himalayan prayer machine, with its humming cylinders and hypnotic cascade of light. It had become a fixture of her days, like the corset, the gloves, the veil.

                                                                                                                                                    “I don’t know,” she said at last. “But I can’t stop.”

                                                                                                                                                    Amara only nodded, her expression unreadable. “Then I will stay and help you.”

                                                                                                                                                    But help changed nothing. Over the following months, Seraphina remained tethered to the prayer machine. The ritual had become compulsive–daily at first, less frequently later, she felt an undeniable need to sit before it, to let its kaleidoscopic light wash over her, to hear the low, vibrating hum. The machine demanded her attention as surely as her wardrobe demanded her restraint.

                                                                                                                                                    The idea of loosening her laces, of dressing without constraint, brought on not relief but suffocating anxiety. Panic followed even the thought of letting go. The habit had burrowed so deep it had become a law of her body.

                                                                                                                                                    Together, she and Amara began to understand that the machine had shaped this transformation–that it was not grief or discipline alone, but something engineered. The machine had conditioned her, insidiously, to live by the Major’s preferences. It did not matter that he was gone. His desires remained, encoded in her body like script carved into stone.

                                                                                                                                                    Even in death, he held her.

                                                                                                                                                    Voyage to England#

                                                                                                                                                    It took ten months from the Major’s passing until Seraphina and Amara could finally board a steamer of the British India Steam Navigation Company, bound for Marseilles. She traveled not only as a widow seeking revenge, but also as a woman fully confined in the life her aunt, Mrs. Blackthorn and the Major had built for her.

                                                                                                                                                    Her restrictive garments–corseted, veiled, gloved, and laced–were no longer just burdens, but they were also part of her. The need for the prayer machine had lessened–it called to her now only every few weeks–but the conditioning held fast. It no longer needed to be reinforced. It had become permanent.

                                                                                                                                                    Of all she might have brought from India, she packed only what mattered: her wardrobe–trunks upon trunks of tightly cut gowns, bonnets, corsets, gloves–and the prayer machine itself, unable to give it up, so it stayed, carefully crated and sealed.

                                                                                                                                                    Aboard the steamer, where the ceaseless expanse of ocean mirrored the isolation of her mourning, Seraphina kept mostly to herself as custom demanded from a widow. Her private cabin was her refuge, a space where she could retreat from the world and manage the compulsions that had taken root in her mind. When she ventured out, her mourning attire was meticulously styled, adhering strictly to the reformatory standards ingrained in her. The heavy black gown and tightly laced corset encased her, the weight of the fabric a constant reminder of her constraints. Over this, she wore a cape, its dark folds enveloping her like a shroud, while a bonnet and veil completed her somber ensemble.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina avoided the vibrant social life aboard the ship. Society demanded that shunned the laughter and clatter of deck games, the conviviality of dinners, preferring the quiet of her own company. The only exception she could make was for the captain, a man of understanding and kindness, who extended invitations to his private table, where she dined with him and his wife.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina adjusted her veil as she entered the captain’s dining room. The low murmur of conversation surrounded her, a gentle balm against the silence of her cabin. Captain Moore stood to greet her, a warm smile on his weathered face. Beside him, his wife, Eleanor, extended a hand.

                                                                                                                                                    “Seraphina, it’s lovely to see you,” Eleanor said, her voice kind.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina nodded politely, taking her seat at the table. “Thank you for having me.”

                                                                                                                                                    The meal passed with light conversation. Seraphina listened to tales of distant ports and nautical adventures. She sipped her soup delicately, responding with appropriate remarks when addressed. Eleanor’s gentle questions drew Seraphina out, engaging her in talk of England and the changes she anticipated on her return.

                                                                                                                                                    “You must be eager to see London again,” Eleanor remarked, offering a sympathetic smile.

                                                                                                                                                    “Yes, though it seems daunting,” Seraphina replied, her fingers brushing the edge of her veil.

                                                                                                                                                    Captain Moore nodded, his expression understanding. “You’ll find your way, I’m sure of it. You have strength, Mrs. Hartwell.”

                                                                                                                                                    The evening ended with Eleanor promising to lend Seraphina a book, a gesture of friendship that warmed the young widow’s heart. These occasions offered the rare human contact, a reminder of the social grace she could still wield effortlessly when needed.

                                                                                                                                                    As the steamer eased into the harbor of Aden, Seraphina stood at the rail, the heat already rising in waves off the basalt shore. The port shimmered with dust and sunlight, a scatter of low white buildings clustered against the jagged hills beyond. Dhows and harbor boats rocked gently near the quay, their sails furled, while darker-hulled steam launches hissed as they moved cargo and passengers to and from the anchored vessels.

                                                                                                                                                    She had informed the ship’s purser the night before of her intent to send a telegram upon arrival. As such, a junior officer, Mr. Travers, a well-mannered midshipman in crisp tropical whites–was assigned to escort her to the telegraph office near the quay.

                                                                                                                                                    The launch bumped softly against the pier. Seraphina stepped ashore carefully, her black crepe skirts gathered slightly in one gloved hand. The soles of her heeled boots clicked sharply on the sun-baked stone of the landing. Her veil, a gauzy cloud of black netting, softened the harsh glare of the tropical sun, but not the heat. The corset beneath her gown pressed firmly against her ribs, and the high collar trapped the warmth like a chimney pot.

                                                                                                                                                    Around her, the harbor bustled with colonial life. Gurkha guards patrolled in pairs. Laborers in light cotton shirts hauled crates from the docks to the bonded warehouses nearby. A pair of Arab boys sat on the steps of a shipping agent’s office, watching the disembarking passengers with bright, curious eyes. The scent of coal smoke, brine, and sweat hung thick in the air.

                                                                                                                                                    She walked steadily, the junior officer just ahead, clearing a path through the bustling people on the quay. The Post & Telegraph Office, marked by a painted wooden sign, stood near the edge of the port compound, its low colonnaded façade shaded by a canvas awning. Inside, the air was marginally cooler. A fan turned lazily overhead, and a clerk in shirtsleeves stood behind the counter, wiping his brow.

                                                                                                                                                    The officer tipped his hat to the man. “Message for England.”

                                                                                                                                                    The clerk looked up, took in Seraphina’s figure–her mourning dress, gloves, the veil–and offered a polite nod. “Yes, ma’am. Just here, if you please.”

                                                                                                                                                    She stepped forward, her posture impeccable despite the sweat gathering at the base of her neck. “I wish to send a telegram to London.”

                                                                                                                                                    The man slid a blank form and pencil across the counter. “One shilling and sixpence for the first ten words, ma’am. One penny for each word after.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina retrieved a small notecase from her reticule and counted out the coins with gloved fingers. She paused, then wrote:

                                                                                                                                                    “To: Mr. Edwin Hawthorne, Carey Street, London. Arrival within fortnight STOP Kindly arrange discreet lodgings STOP Shall call upon arrival STOP Hartwell.”

                                                                                                                                                    The clerk read it back to her slowly, confirming each phrase, then nodded. “That’ll go with the three o’clock signal dispatch, ma’am. Should be in London by nightfall, their time.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Thank you,” she said, her voice soft behind the veil.

                                                                                                                                                    As she turned to leave, the officer offered her his arm. The sun outside struck her like a furnace, but she kept her head high. Behind her, the clatter of the telegraph key had already begun.

                                                                                                                                                    The journey continued, with the ship pressing northward to Suez. It would continue its journey to Marseilles via Port Said, and would linger in port at Suez before continuing, so Seraphina chose a different route. The ship slowed as it approached Suez, and along the low, dusty banks, minarets and palm trees shimmered in the heat haze. Seraphina stood on the shaded deck beneath her black parasol, her gloved hand resting on the railing. Beads of sweat trickled down her spine, caught by the linen lining of her bodice, and the damp edge of her veil clung to her cheeks.

                                                                                                                                                    When the ship finally dropped anchor near the quay, the same Mr. Travers, who had escorted her ashore in Aden, appeared again to assist her. He offered a crisp salute, then guided her carefully down the gangway where the chaos of the port awaited. Steam and camel dung, the shouts of men, the hiss of coal-fired engines, and the sharp tang of salt and spice in the air. The heat was thicker here, and the very dust seemed to cling.

                                                                                                                                                    A squad of sailors had already been dispatched to manage her luggage, the dark trunks marked with the Hartwell name heaved down onto carts. Seraphina moved slowly, flanked by Amara, her face hidden behind the thick veil of mourning, her posture a perfect column of black crepe and restraint.

                                                                                                                                                    At the rail terminus, the platform was a blur of movement. A hundred voices competed–European travelers shouting in English and French, local porters calling out offers in Arabic, the rhythmic bark of train whistles piercing through it all. Steam rose from the waiting locomotive, curling white and ghostly above the polished brass of its boiler.

                                                                                                                                                    A representative from Thomas Cook, neatly dressed and holding a ledger, stepped forward from the crowd. “Mrs. Hartwell?” he asked, bowing slightly. “Your carriage has been prepared.”

                                                                                                                                                    The midshipman handed her over with a deferential nod. “Godspeed, ma’am.” Seraphina inclined her head beneath her veil. “Thank you, Mr. Travers.”

                                                                                                                                                    With practiced efficiency, the Cook agent guided her through the bustle. Her private first-class compartment gleamed in the Egyptian sun, its lacquered wood panels and pale green upholstery offering a vision of cool repose amid the heat. A pair of ceiling fans spun gently from the overhead brass rail, and a white-jacketed porter held the door as Amara stepped in behind her.

                                                                                                                                                    The moment Seraphina settled onto the velvet bench, her shoulders relaxed by the barest degree. The sound of the outside world faded. The thick glass windows dampened the din of the platform. Amara removed Seraphina’s outer veil and straightened her collar with practiced fingers.

                                                                                                                                                    At last, the locomotive gave a long whistle, and with a judder, the train lurched forward. The rush of movement pushed air through the corridor, and for the first time in hours, a true breeze stirred the heavy folds of Seraphina’s gown. She leaned back and allowed her gloved fingers to rest in her lap. Her boots remained pinched, her corset unyielding–but still, the breeze felt like reprieve.

                                                                                                                                                    The service was impeccable. A tray arrived shortly after departure bearing chilled water, slices of lime, and delicately wrapped sandwiches. The uniformed porter offered them silently, his gaze never lingering. Seraphina accepted a glass with a faint nod, and drank slowly.

                                                                                                                                                    Cairo came into view hours later, the city rising from the desert in domes and minarets. The train slowed for the stopover–another convenience arranged by Cook. From her shaded window, she glimpsed crowded marketplaces, carriages drawn by white horses, the faint outline of the citadel in the smoky distance. But she did not disembark. It was enough, for now, to rest, to breathe, to move forward. Alexandria awaited.

                                                                                                                                                    The sea beyond Alexandria was a brilliant, ceaseless blue as Seraphina stood once again at the rail, her black veil tugging gently in the sea breeze. The steamer, a British India vessel on express service to Southampton, pulled away from the harbor with the deep, sonorous call of its whistle. Below, the harbor fell away in layers of sandstone buildings, pale minarets, and the distant shimmer of the sea wall. Her trunks were stowed and her own berth arranged to her liking–though little of her attire could be altered, even at sea.

                                                                                                                                                    In the heat of the lower Mediterranean, her mourning ensemble was once again a trial. The shade of her bonnet provided little relief; her sleeves clung damply to her arms beneath her gloves, and the bodice of her gown, tightly shaped over her cinched waist, held every breath in check.

                                                                                                                                                    She spent most of her time inside the saloon or in her private cabin, the shutters drawn and fans turning slowly. When she did emerge to take air, it was in the early morning or late evening, when the sun hung lower and the breeze off the water cooled the decks.

                                                                                                                                                    Days passed like this, slow and dreamlike, until the ship passed Gibraltar and pushed northwards into the Atlantic. There, the sea changed. The color dulled from sapphire to slate. The wind stiffened and grew colder, carrying with it a rawness that clung to the skin. Seraphina stood on deck again, gloved hands resting on the painted rail, her shoulders wrapped in her mourning shawl. The breeze caught the hem of her skirts and teased her veil outward in a soft arc. For the first time in weeks, the constrictions of her dress felt tolerable. Her corset still pressed in hard around her ribs, her neck collar remained stiff and upright–but the oppressive weight of heat had lifted.

                                                                                                                                                    The sea darkened day by day, and the ship rolled more heavily in the Atlantic swell. Rain came often, brief but insistent, slapping across the deck and sending passengers scurrying inside. Seraphina remained still, weathering it under the canopy, eyes fixed on the horizon, letting the rhythm of the voyage carry her thoughts forward.

                                                                                                                                                    When at last the ship arrived at Southampton, it was under a low sky mottled in iron gray. The port was bustling and wet, the cobbles slick with rain, the air sharp and smelling of coal smoke, wet wood, and brine. The chill crept under her skirts and gloves the moment she stepped ashore, but she welcomed it. After years in India, and months wrapped in black, she found comfort in the gray. The cold felt clean. An ending, or perhaps a beginning.

                                                                                                                                                    The LSWR station was only a short drive from the docks, and her transfer had been arranged in advance. Porters loaded her trunks onto the platform as she and Amara were escorted to a reserved carriage at the front of the train. The interior was warm and quiet, the seats upholstered in burgundy velvet, a faint scent of wax polish in the air. The windows were fogged with condensation, and beyond them the countryside passed–hedgerows, bare-limbed trees, damp fields striped with plough marks, the odd stone cottage drifting by like memories. Seraphina sat still as the train pulled north toward London, her hands folded in her lap, her veil drawn down.

                                                                                                                                                    Hours later, the city appeared–first in chimney stacks and clusters of factory roofs, then in the denser thicket of buildings, the brick lanes streaked with soot and coal dust. London was colder than Southampton, grayer still, and grimy with a thousand fires. The clouds hung low over the skyline.

                                                                                                                                                    London#

                                                                                                                                                    The train hissed into Waterloo with a long exhale of steam. Outside, carriages and hansoms rolled through puddles. Seraphina stepped down with Amara at her side and a porter already reaching for her trunks. Mr. Hawthorne awaited her arrival. He stood tall and dignified, dressed in a dark coat with a discreet mourning band on his arm, a gesture of respect for her loss. A maid accompanied him, her presence a subtle acknowledgment of the social proprieties that governed their interactions. As Seraphina and Amara disembarked, her movements measured beneath the layers of her mourning dress, Mr. Hawthorne greeted her with a polite bow.

                                                                                                                                                    “Mrs. Hartwell,” he said, his voice warm but formal. “It’s a pleasure to see you safely returned to England.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina inclined her head, her veil obscuring her features but not the poise that radiated from her. “Thank you, Mr. Hawthorne. Your assistance has been invaluable.”

                                                                                                                                                    He had arranged for a closed carriage, part of the hotel service, to convey her to The Marlborough Hotel in Bloomsbury. The ride through the bustling streets of London was a swift one, the city a blur of stone and smoke beyond her veil. Porters attended to her luggage with quiet efficiency upon their arrival, and she was soon shown to a room with a view of the garden.

                                                                                                                                                    The hotel was newly built and modern, even if the decorations in her room were a bit on the spartan side. Nevertheless, it was well-heated against the chill of the English climate and comfortable. A large wardrobe stood against one wall, ready to house her extensive wardrobe. A writing desk, situated near the window, would serve as her command center, a place from which she could pen letters and coordinate the next steps of her plans. The en-suite washroom, a welcome luxury, offered a measure of privacy, and the attached maid’s chamber was a great convenience.

                                                                                                                                                    As she moved to the window, her gaze drifting over the garden below, Seraphina allowed herself a moment of reflection. Her first objective upon returning to London was clear: The downfall of Lady Agatha Thistledown and her husband, Lord Thistledown. They had taken her freedom and thrust her into the reformatory, where her life had been molded to their desires. She intended to ruin Agatha’s life, and she already knew how.

                                                                                                                                                    During her travels, Seraphina kept herself informed through the latest issues of the Pall Mall Gazette and, more importantly, the Illustrated London News. The latter, with its richly illustrated society gossip, offered insights into fashion, society, and trends. It was through these pages that Seraphina learned about the rising stars of London society and those whose fortunes were on the decline. One such figure was Kemal Bey, a diplomat of the Ottoman Empire known for his charm and captivating presence. Born into a once-prominent family in Istanbul, Kemal’s fortunes had waned due to his family’s financial mismanagement. His increasingly precarious financial situation had embroiled him in several scandalous situations, making him the perfect tool for her plan against Agatha.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina contacted a discreet private investigator recommended to her by Mr. Hawthorne, a man named Matthew Pike. Pike was a former member of the military police in the British Army, honorably discharged after a bayonet injury in Sudan. He now ran a private inquiry office in Lambeth. While described to her as a good man, he was also recommended for his ability to handle rougher situations. When he met with Seraphina, he greeted her with a respectful “ma’am,” striving to maintain a veneer of upper-class English, though his speech occasionally slipped into more familiar tones.

                                                                                                                                                    “Mr. Pike,” Seraphina began, “I require a file on Kemal Bey, the Ottoman diplomat. I need detailed information on his current circumstances and a way to contact him discreetly.”

                                                                                                                                                    Pike nodded, his gaze sharp and focused. “Of course, ma’am. I’ll gather what I can. Give me a few days.”

                                                                                                                                                    True to his word, Pike returned with a dossier on Kemal Bey. He reported that Kemal was indeed in a precarious financial situation, burdened by debts and desperately seeking ways to improve his fortunes. He had been gambling on horse races, hoping for a windfall to pull him out of his financial woes. His situation made him susceptible to influence–just the opportunity Seraphina needed.

                                                                                                                                                    “Kemal Bey is in deep, ma’am,” Pike explained, his voice low. “He’s charming, no doubt, but he’s got creditors breathing down his neck. He’s been trying his luck at the racetracks, putting his coin on the ponies, trying to win big. It’s a risky game, but he’s got little choice.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina listened intently, her mind already formulating the next steps. “And contact? How do I arrange a meeting without attracting undue attention?”

                                                                                                                                                    Pike handed her a slip of paper with an address and a time. “He’s often at that café, ma’am. You’ll find him there most afternoons, nursing a coffee and a racing form. Approach him then, and you’ll have your chance.”

                                                                                                                                                    Ascot#

                                                                                                                                                    While Pike’s information was useful, Seraphina resolved to pursue a more direct strategy. She intended to approach Kemal Bey herself–but on her terms, and with her timing. Ascot presented the perfect opportunity. She was confident he would be there, gambling on horses in a last-ditch effort to chase solvency. He would be easy to spot, his face known to her from his frequent appearances in the Illustrated London News.

                                                                                                                                                    Before setting out, she penned an elegant, anonymous note on cream-laid paper, the ink drying quickly in the warm spring air. The message was concise:

                                                                                                                                                    “Mr. Bey, it has come to my attention that we may share mutual interests. I propose we meet at noon in the British Museum, by the Assyrian Lamassu statues, to discuss a matter of some importance.”

                                                                                                                                                    For her incognito visit, Seraphina abandoned her mourning attire in favor of a more discreet ensemble. Yet even out of black, her style remained strict and refined. She wore a gown of light green faille, its surface matte and modest, trimmed only with subtle piping at the seams. The tight bodice was laced as tightly as it would go, shaping her into a wasp-waisted silhouette that moved with deliberate grace. The skirt was slim, tapering just enough to demand mincing steps; each stride was short and composed. Her posture collar, rigid and high, cradled her jaw and lifted her chin, allowing no downward glance. A wide-brimmed hat veiled her face in fine black netting, its edge falling far over her shoulders.

                                                                                                                                                    The Royal Ascot grounds were a vivid spectacle of Victorian England at its most theatrical.

                                                                                                                                                    The wealthy and titled moved through the enclosures like costumed players, their laughter bright beneath the white marquees. The gentlemen wore morning coats in black or dove gray, the better sort pairing them with silk top hats and ivory-handled walking sticks. Some wore carnations in their buttonholes and smoked Turkish cigarettes in slender ivory holders. Their conversation flowed easily–from bloodlines of horses to politics at Westminster–spoken in clipped tones over silver flasks of brandy or champagne served in cut crystal.

                                                                                                                                                    The women, their dresses ablaze with color and ruffles, swirled in an ocean of silk, lace, and tulle. Parasols bloomed like flowers against the afternoon sun. They clustered near the grandstand and paddock rails, whispering names of favored jockeys and laughing softly behind gloved fingers. Feathered hats rose like architectural marvels above their heads, some teetering with absurd hauteur.

                                                                                                                                                    Outside the Royal Enclosure, however, the social palette darkened. Here, tradesmen, clerks, and milliners rubbed shoulders with betting touts, hawkers, and pickpockets. Ragged boys with ink-smudged faces shouted the latest odds, while stout women sold penny broadsheets listing race entries from baskets slung over their arms. Vendors offered meat pies, roasted chestnuts, and gin cups with cracked ice. The lower classes congregated around the rails, craning to catch a glimpse of the thoroughbreds or cheering with almost superstitious devotion as the horses thundered past.

                                                                                                                                                    In one corner, a knot of cockney bookmakers barked out odds, their flat caps bobbing as they shouted over one another. Beside them, a nervous young man in a cheap morning coat fumbled a few coins onto a race stub and hurried away, eyes wide.

                                                                                                                                                    Children darted between skirts and boots, some with baskets of violets for sale, others pilfering coins when they thought no one was looking. The scent of horses, cigar smoke, and hot pastry clung to the air.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina moved among them like a ghost–veiled, composed, and utterly apart. She kept to the periphery of the grandstand, her veiled gaze sweeping across the crowd. It did not take long to spot Kemal Bey.

                                                                                                                                                    He was dressed with the studied elegance of a man striving to project affluence–his chocolate-brown cutaway coat just a little too worn at the seams, the camel-coloured waistcoat brushed clean but fraying at the edge, and a dove-gray cravat, crumbled and straightened. A modest rosebud adorned his buttonhole, and a silver watch chain swung from his waistcoat with the casual flair of someone hoping it might distract from the threadbare quality of their outfit.

                                                                                                                                                    He leaned against the rail, a folded racing form in one hand, his eyes fixed intently on the betting boards. The concentration etched across his face was too grave, too purposeful, for a gentleman meant merely to be enjoying an afternoon’s leisure.

                                                                                                                                                    From her vantage, Seraphina observed as he entered into a terse exchange with one of the bookmakers. The bookmaker was a bald man with a reddish complexion and no eyebrows, a man known as The Egg to Mr. Pike, and mentioned helpfully in his reports as one of Mr. Bey’s creditors. He stood behind a makeshift chalkboard and spoke in brusque, impatient tones.

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal reached into his coat pocket, produced a handful of banknotes, and hesitated. The sum was evidently insufficient. After a glance around to ensure no one of consequence was watching, he withdrew a card and scribbled something on it–an IOU, by the look of it–then signed it with a flourish. The bookmaker accepted the note with evident relish, his grin thin and wolfish, before handing over the betting slip.

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal took it as though it were a banknote, folding it into his hand with visible care. He turned, his eyes scanning the racetrack with the desperation of a man who had staked more than he could afford. Seraphina, her veil concealing the intensity of her interest, moved with the crowd, keeping a measured distance behind him.

                                                                                                                                                    She glanced at the bookie’s chalkboard. The odds had shifted. The outsider’s numbers had shortened considerably–evidence of a large wager placed at the last moment. She allowed herself a small, inward nod. A risky bet. Foolhardy, even. The kind of gamble one made when there were no safe options left.

                                                                                                                                                    The race was announced, and the crowd surged toward the railings. Kemal stood near the front, his body rigid with anticipation. Seraphina lingered a few paces behind, her posture serene, her gloved hands folded at her waist. She did not watch the track; she watched him.

                                                                                                                                                    As the thunder of hooves erupted and the racers tore past in a blur of motion, she could see the line of his shoulders grow taut. His breathing changed, shallow and quick. And when the favourite surged ahead in the final furlong and crossed the finish to raucous cheers, she saw him sag–his spine bowed, the betting slip crumpled in his fist.

                                                                                                                                                    The moment was perfect.

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina stepped forward without haste. Her gloved hand reached out, light and deliberate, and came to rest upon his forearm. He turned, startled–his expression flushed with disappointment, eyes wide with confusion and fear.

                                                                                                                                                    She slipped the envelope into his hand. Her voice, low and precise beneath the veil, followed like a blade.

                                                                                                                                                    “Tomorrow,” she said softly, her tone firm, with the faintest edge of command. “By the Lamassu. Be there.”

                                                                                                                                                    Before he could respond, before he could even fully process her presence, she had stepped back into the crowd–swallowed by parasols, hats, and colour–leaving only the faintest trace of perfume and paper in her wake, leaving Kemal with nothing but the note and the promise of the next day’s meeting.

                                                                                                                                                    British Museum#

                                                                                                                                                    The following day, Seraphina instructed Amara to dress her in precisely the same attire she had worn to Ascot. She wanted Kemal Bey to recognize her silhouette and the unique constraints of her form. Her thick veil remained in place, ensuring her identity would remain hidden.

                                                                                                                                                    Arriving at the British Museum ahead of their meeting, Seraphina took time to familiarize herself with the Assyrian collection. She found a niche that offered a clear view of the Lamassu statues, allowing her to observe Kemal’s arrival without being seen. The museum’s hushed atmosphere, with its echoing footsteps and distant murmurs, provided the perfect backdrop for their clandestine encounter.

                                                                                                                                                    A few minutes before noon, Kemal Bey entered the room. He glanced around, his demeanor cautious as he positioned himself near the imposing Lamassu bull. When Kemal turned, searching for a sign of his mysterious benefactor, he found her standing just behind him–poised, veiled, and utterly unreadable. She might have stepped out of the shadows themselves, her presence silent yet commanding, as if she belonged not among the living but among the ancient stone sentinels of Assyria that loomed around them.

                                                                                                                                                    “Mr Bey,” she said quietly, her voice calm but unmistakably firm through the fine mesh of her veil. “I trust you received my message.”

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal inclined his head, studying her with a guarded expression. “Indeed,” he said. “And you have my attention. Though I must confess, I am still unsure whether I should be intrigued or alarmed.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina did not flinch. “I can offer you one thousand five hundred pounds,” she said without preamble, her tone precise, her posture impeccable. “It will be enough to settle your debts and remove you from The Egg’s reach. I propose an advance of ten percent–enough to keep your name off his list, and buy you time.”

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal’s eyes narrowed. The number was no small sum. Still, his hesitation was immediate, his thoughts clearly racing.

                                                                                                                                                    “And in return?” he asked slowly, already suspecting the answer.

                                                                                                                                                    “You will seduce Lady Thistledown,” Seraphina said, her voice even. “And you will ensure that you are discovered. The disgrace must be public. Her ruin must be absolute.”

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal drew a sharp breath and turned away for a moment, one gloved hand pressed to his lips as if to keep the words from spilling. He began pacing–a short, agitated loop in front of the Lamassu.

                                                                                                                                                    “That would end her, yes,” he said at last, almost to himself. “But it would end me as well. No respectable embassy would touch me after such a scandal. My position in London would become untenable. I’d be left with nothing but exile and debt.”

                                                                                                                                                    “You would be free,” Seraphina replied, her voice low, persuasive but unyielding. “Free of your creditors. Free of the whispers. You could leave this city behind. Begin again–in Istanbul, or wherever your name has not yet reached. With your dignity intact. With your future paid for.”

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal stopped pacing and turned back to her, the tension in his frame visible even beneath his well-cut coat. “Even so, it’s not enough,” he said slowly. “Not for that. You’re asking me to leap from a high window without knowing what’s below.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Two thousand,” Seraphina said crisply. “In full. When the Lady is compromised and the scandal is published.”

                                                                                                                                                    He let out a soft, humourless breath. “That barely covers what I owe,” he said. “And what then? I crawl back to Constantinople in disgrace, with no post, no patron, and no prospects?”

                                                                                                                                                    “You’ll be free of the worst of them,” Seraphina said calmly. “You’ll walk away from The Egg with your skin and your name. That is more than most men manage.” She paused. “Two thousand five hundred,” she said, the tone of finality in her voice.

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal shook his head, slowly. “It’s not enough. I’ll need more than just my debts paid. I’ll need to live. I’ll need to eat, and work, and dress like a man not entirely fallen.” He paused, looking at her from beneath his lashes. “Three thousand five hundred.”

                                                                                                                                                    Seraphina tilted her head slightly, considering him from behind the veil. Then, with no further expression, she turned slightly–enough to suggest she was preparing to leave.

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal’s voice cracked with urgency. “Three, then,” he said, the words leaving his mouth in a rush. “Three thousand. Not a penny less. And I’ll do it.”

                                                                                                                                                    She paused, still turned half away, then looked back at him through the gauze of her veil. For a long moment, neither of them moved.

                                                                                                                                                    Then Seraphina reached into her reticule and withdrew three tightly bound packets of banknotes, each precisely folded and tied. She held them out. “Ten percent,” she said. “Three hundred pounds. Enough to buy silence and a stay of execution from your creditors. The remainder when the scandal breaks. When the Lady falls.”

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal accepted the envelope, the notes suddenly weightier than gold. His jaw worked silently. He looked down at the packet, then slipped it into the inside pocket of his coat.

                                                                                                                                                    Without another word, Seraphina inclined her head once and turned, her footsteps soft as she disappeared between the shadows of the towering Assyrian statues.

                                                                                                                                                    Kemal stood alone in the gallery. The great stone Lamassu watched him in silence, their eyes fixed and unreadable, guardians of a long-dead empire. He reached into his pocket and felt the paper crinkle–the money, the promise, the price. He looked up at the carved kings and whispered, half to himself, half to them.

                                                                                                                                                    “So be it.”

                                                                                                                                                    Then he turned and walked away.

                                                                                                                                                    This wasn’t a school. It was a factory. And “Advanced Therapy” wasn’t education—it was correction. Reform, maybe. Or erasure.

                                                                                                                                                    She looked around the room again, newly unsettled by the silence, by the neatness. Whoever ran this place had done so with purpose. And someone—several someones—had paid for it. Encouraged it. Expected it. Handed over their daughters who did not conform to the standards their families demanded, reshaped into model women fit for marriage, society, or silence.

                                                                                                                                                    Claire exhaled slowly, the air thick in her lungs. She closed the ledger gently, as if shutting the book might muffle what she’d just learned. It didn’t.

                                                                                                                                                    A second book in that drawer was obviously a diary. She flipped forward, past faded ink, until she came to a marked entry near the middle.

                                                                                                                                                    August 17th, 1899

                                                                                                                                                    Today marks the final settlement of accounts with the Hargreaves estate. With the Rear Admiral’s passing and no male heir to contest, I have at last secured full ownership of the institution. Lord Everingham proved gracious, as ever—his patronage remains vital, but he sees the wisdom in unified leadership. A school cannot thrive by committee. Continuity has been preserved. I have written to the solicitor confirming the dissolution of the founding consortium. I hold the deeds myself now. The reformatory’s future is mine to shape, without compromise, without interference.

                                                                                                                                                    May it remain a place of discipline and order in an era increasingly given over to indulgence. I shall carry forward its mission—refined, yes, but unswerving.

                                                                                                                                                    Claire frowned. She flipped to the front, toward the earliest entries, and read.

                                                                                                                                                    January 2nd, 1885 It is not without some heaviness of heart that I accept the burden placed upon me today. The reformatory was never my ambition, nor do I imagine myself a natural schoolmistress. But with my husband’s affairs concluded, and my situation pressing as it is, I cannot afford discretion. And yet I do not despair.

                                                                                                                                                    With Mrs. Blackthorn’s… departure—at last—and with her, I pray, the cruelty she so thinly veiled as correction. I will run the school differently. Not softly, but justly. The girls must be guided, not broken. Restrained, but not humiliated. There is dignity in discipline if meted with compassion.

                                                                                                                                                    This place can change them for the better, I am sure of it. It need only change its heart first.

                                                                                                                                                    Claire closed the book.

                                                                                                                                                    It didn’t add up. That first voice—tentative, almost tender—was a world away from the cold precision of the ledger. Somewhere between that winter and the turn of the century, Mrs. Hartwell had changed. Or the school had. Or both.

                                                                                                                                                    And whatever “Advanced Therapy” was, it hadn’t been born of compassion. Claire needed to understand what had truly happened in this place.

                                                                                                                                                    Her gaze drifted back to the box.

                                                                                                                                                    It didn’t belong here. Not in a Victorian stone mansion tucked away in the Scottish highlands. The box was made of dark, polished wood—almost black, with a lacquered sheen—and carved all over in intricate, swirling patterns that shimmered faintly when the light hit them. Not floral. Not quite geometric. Almost… alive. The corners were inlaid with what looked like jade, cool and green, while the panels shimmered with embedded stones: turquoise, lapis, violet glass, flakes of gold. Delicate scrolls and cylinders were integrated into the design, like a music box crossed with something religious. The whole thing radiated presence. Not just exotic—ritualistic. As if it had come from somewhere older, deeper, and far from here.

                                                                                                                                                    Claire stepped forward, holding her breath, and laid both hands on the lid. It opened like a triptych. The outer panels folded back with a soft click, revealing a strange, layered interior: brass rods, interlocked gears, scrollwork spindles, and etched plates that caught the sunlight like fire.

                                                                                                                                                    She had no idea what it was.

                                                                                                                                                    But she was certain it had been used. Many times.

                                                                                                                                                    She turned one of the cylinders. It moved with a gentle resistance, then clicked into place.

                                                                                                                                                    A hum began. Low and deep. It settled into her chest like distant thunder rolling through stone. Behind the embedded gems, lights began to stir—faint at first, then brightening into a full cascade of motion and color. Sapphire, emerald, violet, gold—reflections spun across the walls and floor like living prisms gone wild.

                                                                                                                                                    Claire watched.

                                                                                                                                                    She meant to look away. She even tried, just for a second. To laugh. To mutter “What the hell is this thing?” But she didn’t. The lights pulsed in slow rhythm, each wave of color drawing her deeper inward. The hum settled into her bones. Her breath slowed.

                                                                                                                                                    Her shoulders lowered.

                                                                                                                                                    The buzz of thought began to fade, smoothed into stillness. Behind her eyes, the tightness unwound.

                                                                                                                                                    She sat down without thinking—on the edge of a trunk, or maybe a stool—her gaze still locked on the machine. It moved in delicate motion now, cylinders turning, light shifting in time. She couldn’t look away. Didn’t want to.

                                                                                                                                                    Time stuttered, then vanished.

                                                                                                                                                    The light seemed to fill the space where thought had been. Her limbs were heavy. Her mind, warm. The outside world dulled into quiet.

                                                                                                                                                    She blinked once.

                                                                                                                                                    The machine continued. The lights shimmered. The hum vibrated low and sweet.

                                                                                                                                                    She didn’t move.

                                                                                                                                                    And then—softly, like a thought not quite hers—came a voice beneath the hum, a whisper inside the stillness:

                                                                                                                                                    You don’t have to think right now. Just listen.

# Living In A Changed Society

Several weeks into her effort to blend in, Sonia discovered that blending in was not the same thing as pretending.

Pretending had edges. It required attention.

This was becoming easier than attention.

She noticed it first at night.

The day corset came off when she got home, but not into a heap on the floor the way she had stripped it off in Shanghai. Now she changed into the softer night corset almost automatically, fingers moving through the laces with the dull competence of repetition. The first evening she had done it for comfort she had sworn at herself. By the fifth or sixth, she had stopped swearing. By the second week, going to bed uncorseted made her feel oddly unfinished, too loose in the middle, as if her body had been asked to hold its own shape without enough guidance.

That should have humiliated her.

Instead it made her more careful to keep the night corset within easy reach.

Mornings were worse.

Or better. Depending on what exactly was being measured.

She could dress herself now without the process turning into a fight. Gowbao Yoohoo blouse. Corset. Skirt or dress cut narrow enough to manage her stride. Gloves. Shoes. Mask when the day required it. Hair pinned. The sequence no longer felt alien. Not natural, exactly. Fluent. That was closer. The city had become easier to move through once she presented herself in its language, and her body had started treating that ease as reward.

At the tram stop one wet Monday morning, a gust of wind drove cold rain under the shelter roof. Sonia shifted the paper folder in her hands and tried to angle herself so the droplets would not spot the pages. A man beside her, older, umbrella already open, stepped closer without crowding.

"Here, miss."

He tilted the umbrella just far enough to cover both her and the folder.

Sonia thanked him automatically, with the small lowered head and contained gratitude that no longer had to be invented on the spot. He nodded once as if the exchange had gone exactly as it ought to and turned back toward the tracks.

Nothing in it was dramatic.

That was the thing.

The city kept offering her these tiny arrangements of dependence, each one too minor to protest without sounding absurd. Men moved chairs before she sat. Held doors while her skirt and heels made speed unattractive. Reached the top shelf in a bookshop without asking whether she needed help because the answer, in her clothes, was already visible. Once, in the square outside GAIA, a young man she had never seen before caught her elbow as she stepped off a curb slick with rain and steadied her so neatly that she barely had time to be startled before she was thanking him.

Every single thing could be dismissed on its own.

Taken together, they formed an atmosphere.

At work that atmosphere had become institutional.

GAIA had not turned into Shanghai. It had turned into a place that assumed women like Sonia would be corseted, masked some of the time, gloved most of the time, and not especially well served by ordinary office furniture or casual meeting etiquette. Chairs with higher backs had appeared in conference spaces. Walkways for summer events had been laid with heel-friendly surfaces. Meetings ran more slowly and more deliberately when women were masked, less because anyone claimed to be accommodating than because the men chairing them had quietly started building women’s altered presentation into the structure of professional life.

The first time Sonia admitted that some of it worked better this way, she nearly walked the thought back out of her own head.

It happened during a strategy meeting.

Three men from finance, two from policy, Sonia, and another senior woman from operations. Ten months earlier the room would have filled with people talking over each other and then pretending afterward that the strongest ideas had simply risen to the top. Instead, the discussion moved in set turns. The men spoke aloud. Sonia and the other woman contributed through hand-dancing because both were masked that morning, and the men stopped, watched, translated aloud for the notes, then answered. The signed contributions did not feel lesser or provisional. The language was exact, expressive, and impossible to interrupt in the old way. No interruptions. No one barging through a point because he had found a louder one. No need for Sonia to fight to keep the floor while simultaneously managing how angry or imprecise her own voice might sound.

She hated how much easier it was to think inside that arrangement.

Afterward Henry walked beside her down the corridor with the unforced patience that had become his way.

"You were right about the compliance model," he said. "I hadn't accounted for municipal delay."

Sonia glanced at him.

*All that, you understood?*

"Not all of it." He smiled. "You sign faster when you're annoyed."

She should have been irritated by how closely he had learned to read her.

Instead the comment warmed her in a place she did not entirely trust.

*That, is it a complaint?*

"Not remotely."

He opened a door before she reached it. Not ostentatiously. Just in time.

The small grace of that followed her all the way back to her office.

The office itself had changed too, though less visibly. Sonia had switched to a kneeling stool because it worked better with her skirt than any standard chair. At first that had felt like capitulation. Now it felt like practicality. Typing in gloves, another indignity that had once left her furious, had become manageable after she learned the exact pressure each key required and stopped taking the gloves off every time she needed precision. The first day she worked bare-handed again for half an hour and then had to pause because the exposure felt wrong: skin on keys, skin on communal surfaces, skin on the world.

That bothered her enough that she pulled the gloves back on with a muttered curse and could not decide which part of the reaction she found more embarrassing.

Even lunch had become social in a way she had not chosen but could no longer ignore.

Masked women did not simply eat at their desks anymore. They moved toward the privacy cabins or toward small side rooms and alcoves that everyone now treated as obvious infrastructure. Sonia had resisted at first, resenting the inconvenience and the symbolism both. Then one afternoon she found herself in a cabin with two women from policy, all three of them unmasked in the little hush of the enclosed space, eating soup and sharing the kind of easy professional gossip that never seemed to happen when she was left alone with a tray and a laptop.

One of them mentioned her guardian. The other complained affectionately about her brother reorganizing her bookshelves without asking. They laughed. Sonia laughed with them. For ten minutes the whole thing felt less like social engineering than like belonging to a world with extra rules and better manners.

She left the cabin annoyed by how pleasant it had been.

The city had also become startlingly skilled at smoothing over the practical humiliations of feminine restriction. At a summer reception in the GAIA courtyard, wooden planks had been laid across the grass so that heels would not sink. High-backed chairs stood in clusters where women in narrow skirts could perch without struggling. Men moved around the edges of those arrangements adjusting, assisting, anticipating, never quite making a performance of it. Sonia watched one young intern in a severe dark dress step toward a chair after an hour on her feet, only for a man from legal to appear, set the chair exactly where she needed it, and disappear again before thanks could become a scene.

The intern sat with a look of relieved dignity Sonia understood too well.

Convenience was becoming inseparable from submission.

Not philosophically.

Practically.

That distinction mattered less every week.

The sharpest version of it struck on a Thursday evening in a crowded bookstore. Sonia was reaching for a volume on a high shelf more out of reflex than realism when Henry appeared beside her.

"Which one?"

She pointed.

He took it down, checked the cover, and handed it to her without comment.

*This, you keep doing,* she signed.

"Helping?"

*Appearing at the exact moment.*

His mouth shifted with amusement.

"I work in the same building. I frequent the same tram lines. We shop in the same district. It's not quite supernatural."

Sonia held the book against her ribs.

*Even so.*

Henry's gaze dropped for the briefest moment to the line of her corset under the jacket, then back to her face.

"You notice things more when you need people," he said.

That was an outrageous sentence. Condescending, maybe. Intimate, certainly.

It was also true enough to leave her without an immediate answer.

He opened the shop door for her on the way out. She passed through it with a quiet thank-you and spent the next block angry at herself for feeling accompanied rather than diminished.

That was the real problem taking shape under all the smaller ones.

She had spent years understanding autonomy as the absence of dependence. The ability to move, speak, choose, and act without waiting on anyone else's permission, timing, or body. Changed society was making a competing offer, not in slogans but in daily life. What if dependence, arranged correctly, did not feel like weakness from the inside? What if it felt like being anticipated, supported, carried, absorbed into a choreography that prevented the little abrasions of ordinary life before they happened?

On bad days the whole thing still looked like a polished cage.

On other days it looked like care.

One evening she stood in front of the mirror after work, still corseted, still gloved, mask set aside on the dresser, and studied what had become ordinary. The dress held her upright. The shoulder line was clean. Her waist looked smaller than nature had intended. Every visible choice had already been narrowed by the code of the world outside her door.

She should have looked at herself and seen only coercion.

She did see it.

She also saw coherence.

The second recognition was the one that frightened her.

By then she had started asking Anna more pointed questions about daily life. Not ideology. Not whether the system was just. Anna answered those too, with all the sincerity Sonia had come to expect, but that was not what Sonia wanted most. She wanted logistics. Sensation. What it felt like never to move through the world alone, what correction did to the body, what praise did, what happened when a woman stopped fighting the support structures built around her and let them hold.

Those were not academic questions anymore.

She knew that.

At home, alone, the apartment often felt larger than it really was. Too quiet. Too unstructured. Public life had become crowded with little dependencies, and private life answered that by feeling empty rather than free. Sonia would take off the mask, loosen the day corset, change into the softer one for evening, and feel the silence close around her like a missing step.

She was not ready to call that loneliness.

She was getting uncomfortably close.

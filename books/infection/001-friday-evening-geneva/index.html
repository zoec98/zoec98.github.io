                                                                                                                                                  Storycodes

                                                                                                                                                  M/f, F/f, bdsm, con, mind-control, transformation, submission, body-modification, humiliation, conditioning, corruption, latex, retro-femininity, dystopia

                                                                                                                                                  A mysterious virus reshapes women’s bodies and minds into submissive fantasies, rewriting their desires and identities.

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia and Anna, two brilliant scientists in Geneva, discover that the strange viral samples they retrieved are not natural at all but engineered with an unknown purpose. When infection overtakes them, their fight against fever and hallucinations becomes a struggle against the virus’s insidious programming. Dreams of obedience, compulsions to dress and act “properly,” and transformations of their bodies push them further from who they once were. With every step into elegant clothing, with every reward for compliance, they feel their resistance weaken. Soon, they must confront the question of whether they are still themselves at all—or whether they have been rewritten into docile, beautiful, submissive creations of the virus.

                                                                                                                                                  Friday Evening, Geneva#

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia flew from Shenzhen back to Geneva. There weren’t any direct flights available, and she had a biohazard container with chilled viral samples to attend to, which flew with her under a special permit. That meant she slept very badly with the container plugged into the airplane power system and chained to her wrist. She arrived in Frankfurt at six in the morning. While immigration and changing planes had been a breeze with her special permit, the sample container prevented her, of course, from wandering around the airport. So she had to sit around in Frankfurt for another six hours of layover, chained to a power outlet and the container, until she could embark on the last leg of her journey. Consequently, she arrived in Geneva tired and with dark shadows under her eyes, her white blouse and charcoal trousers crumpled. At least her black blazer had survived the flight without new creases. Again, getting through the airport was speedy due to the urgent nature of her special luggage and the magic of her permits. Stepping outside the airport, she hopped into a taxi.

                                                                                                                                                  “Campus Biotech,” she commanded the taxi driver, “the GAIA lab, please.” “Sorry, the what lab?” came back. “Genomic Advancements and Innovations Alliance, just follow the signs at the Campus Biotech. It is easy to find,” Sonia answered with an exhausted voice. The driver started the car and carefully moved into the traffic, but this being afternoon on a Friday, the ten kilometer drive turned into a nearly half an hour affair. Especially down by the lake, traffic slowed down to a standstill.

                                                                                                                                                  Eventually they pulled into a parking bay in front of the GAIA lab, a new building made from glass, steel and mirrors on the Campus Biotech. The building sat incongruously between early 1900s buildings and a lakefront park to the east, and the rails of Geneva Secheron station to the west. She swiped her credit card and got out, heading directly into GAIA before even dropping her luggage off at home, wanting nothing more than to get rid of the sample container chained to her wrist. Sonia had done these field trips before, she was GAIA’s specialist for them, but this one was certainly more exhausting than others.

                                                                                                                                                  And more mysterious. LASS-VASH-2024, the LArge Self-Stabilizing Viral Airborne SHenzhen strain, was not Ebola, not even another SARS variant like COVID-19, but it was highly unusual and something that GAIA wanted to have a closer look at. They had sent her on a fishing expedition for something that turned out to produce a case of bad head cold with some nightmares. But while viral genomes are normally telegrams in size, by comparison this thing came with a genome the equivalent of Wikipedia and the Encyclopedia Britannica combined. It should be unstable or mutating rapidly until it fell apart, but it wasn’t.

                                                                                                                                                  In any case, entering the building, swiping her access card and scanning her retina, she got in, headed straight for the elevator array and swiped her card once more. The lift dropped her three floors underground, directly into the sequencing lab that Anna ran.

                                                                                                                                                  The setup looked like something taken straight out of a sci-fi flick. Arrays of PCR machines were paired with AI powered analytics computers for rapid sequencing of unknown viruses. Screens, blinkenlights, some replacement computer parts, chemical analytics equipment, freezers and chillers, and a lone Bunsen burner with a tripod and an asbestos plate, hosting an Erlenmeyer flask. The latter part was used mostly to make Anna’s specialty coffee.

                                                                                                                                                  “Salut ma chérie!” as Anna hurried to greet her, her colorful running shoes squeaking on the lab floor. Anna was in her usual runners casual, black leggings and a fitted long-sleeved top in a soft shade of gray, covered by a lab coat that had seen better days. “Nice to see you again. What do you bring me? Did you get the samples of that monster… Ma fille, tu as une sale tête. Are you okay?”

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia smiled wearily. And yes, she knew she looked like hell. “I didn’t get enough sleep at all. I mean, it was interesting—I sent you emails—but also exhausting. Did I mention that I didn’t get enough sleep?”

                                                                                                                                                  “Come, sit down. Power and cooling for the samples container first, then coffee for you. You need a break. Then spill the story,” Anna said. Sonia finally unlocked the container from her wrist and handed it to Anna, glad to be finally rid of it. Anna took it over to her workbench and plugged it in. The status lights for power switched from battery to grid, and the box chirped happily as it started to charge. Meanwhile, Anna got busy with the bunsen and some dangerous looking contraption of chemistry lab glassware next to her workstation, then plunked down a coffee in front of Sonia. At last, it smelt and looked like coffee.

                                                                                                                                                  “Good thing you didn’t dress in Maylee Shufoo,” Anna commented dryly, looking at Sonia listlessly stirring sugar into her coffee.

                                                                                                                                                  “Well,” Sonia grinned, “it would certainly keep me upright. No, seriously, let’s get this analysis underway, and then I’ll crash at home and sleep for a few days.”

                                                                                                                                                  “Nah,” Anna came back. “Tomorrow brunch at the Lakeside Café, we have a date,” she reminded Sonia.

                                                                                                                                                  She then browsed the documentation that Sonia pulled out of her bags, checked that she could proceed safely, and unboxed the samples from the biohazard container with practiced motions.

                                                                                                                                                  “Sequencers are ready to go,” Anna announced, preparing the samples and carefully placing them into the slots. She adjusted the sequencers and started the process. The machinery started up with a slight whine, calibrating itself and warming up, then the soft hum of stepper motors and pumps filled the lab. Outside and three floors above them, the late spring light slowly faded, and it was getting dark.

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia was leaning against the counter, empty coffee cup in hand and obviously fighting her jet lag. “How long until we get results?” she asked, knowing full well that a virus with such an outsized genome would take some time to analyze. Possibly Monday, by her guess, for a full analysis. Still, she was hanging around a bit to see if Anna could get some early results. She was not wrong: The screen at Anna’s workstation started to show some initial data, as the sequencers brought up some summary results and a large scale overview. It was a small wonder that this virus did not travel as “oversized luggage”, with a genome this outsized. Her guess of “Monday” was proving to be spot on.

                                                                                                                                                  Suddenly Anna mumbled as she found something: “CRISP-Fold.” She identified standard markers, telltale sequences in bioengineering, but their presence in this sample was unsettling. Sonia straightened, suddenly interested despite being tired. “CRISP-Fold? But that’s…” She stopped, realizing the importance of Anna’s find. “This analysis is known to be precise, and here we see CRISP-Fold markers.” Anna confirmed. “It looks like someone has made the virus on purpose. It’s not natural, it has been definitively engineered. But of course we can’t tell from the markers who did it.”

                                                                                                                                                  These markers were leftovers from a genetic synthesis process which never occurred in nature. It was a clear sign that someone had created the LASS-VASH-2024, either from scratch or by combining different parts of existing viral genomes. This was a shock: Was this a bioweapon of some kind, or an experiment that somehow escaped from the lab?

                                                                                                                                                  “But who did it? And why?” Sonia asked nobody in particular.

                                                                                                                                                  Anna shrugged. “We don’t know. The virus is clearly synthetic or a synthetically modified variant of something natural, for an unknown purpose.” But finding these markers was like finding a clue without knowing who it pointed to. They were an artifact of the process, but contained no information on who ran that process. They fell quiet. This far below, there was no other sound but the hum of the machines doing their work. Above them, the city was slowly winding down as darkness fell.

                                                                                                                                                  Then Anna found more and pointed at the screen. “Look at this,” she said. The screen showed sequences that indicated the presence of a mechanism for bypassing the blood-brain-barrier. The virus did not just infect the body, it could also enter the brain and affect changes there. “That could explain the dreams,” Sonia admitted quietly. They looked at each other, worried about what this meant.

                                                                                                                                                  Sonia felt uneasy. The discoveries did not help to put her mind to rest at all. “I’ve been feeling off,” she admitted to Anna. “It may just be the jet lag, but it could also be more. The samples are samples captured during an active outbreak. I have been careful collecting them, standard procedures. But there is always a risk.”

                                                                                                                                                  Anna knew how that part of the work went, even if Sonia was the field agent, and she was the lab rat in their team. She tried to calm her friend. “We can’t test for the LASS-VASH-2024 yet,” she said. “That can only happen after the sequencing is finished, and we have identified potential markers.” Anna’s worry crept into her voice. “I’d say, take it slowly, and keep a diary of how you feel. We know that this thing may be large, but the symptoms are largely harmless. We’re after it because it is so unusually formed, not because it is dangerous in any way. It is supposed to be just a cold, you told me.”

                                                                                                                                                  Writing a protocol was a small thing, but it gave them something to do. “If you’re lucky, it’s just jet lag,” she tried to support Sonia. “If not, at least we’ll have valuable data for science. In any case, you’ll live.”

                                                                                                                                                  Usually, Anna and Sonia dealt with much more dangerous stuff, and they had strict protocols for that. In this case, this was a fishing expedition for something not classified as dangerous, but a nuisance, only interesting because of its highly unusual size. And now the markers. She was going to be lenient on her tired friend. At least somewhat: There still was tomorrow’s date at the Lakeside Café, celebrating Sonia’s return. That was too risky now, of course. “We have to cancel,” she said. “You need to isolate. Stay away from others, just to be safe.” Sonia understood. She was not happy about this change of plans because of a potential virus infection, but they had to be careful.

                                                                                                                                                  “I will walk home with you,” Anna offered. Normally Sonia would take the bus, just for convenience, but it wasn’t that far. Anna would run, naturally. She always did, the distance was barely enough to make her break sweat. Two kilometers from the Campus Biotech to their apartments in Les Grottes, relatively close to each other. Sonia in Rue du Fort-Barreau, Anna two blocks further on, on Rue de Midi, “Hipster Ground Zero.” During their walk, they did not talk much. Instead, they enjoyed the quiet parks, passed by the train station, and then entered Les Grottes. A twenty-minute walk, maybe twenty-five minutes, with Sonia being so worn down and further encumbered by her luggage.

                                                                                                                                                  Anna bid Sonia a good night in front of Sonia’s apartment. “Tu es sûre que ça va aller, Sonia? You are still not looking good.” She did worry about Sonia’s state: Sonia looked tired and slightly sick, but on the other hand, she didn’t have any problems with the walk. Sonia tried to smile back: “Merci, Anna, Sweetie. With luck, it’s only Jet Lag. A good night’s sleep will help, then we will know more.”

                                                                                                                                                  “D’accord. But call me if you need anything or feel worse, will you? It’s only two blocks, I can easily drop in and bring stuff if you need anything, you know?” It would not be the first time they cared for each other, them having studied Biology and Virology together, and partying hard during these times. “Sure thing. You’re too kind.” Sonia replied. “I’ll call in any case tomorrow,” she promised. “Bonne nuit, Anna, and thank you for everything today.” Sonia would probably be okay. “Bonne nuit, Sonia, and take good care of yourself, d’accord?” Anna watched Sonia go inside, then turned to finish the final few blocks to her own place.

                                                                                                                                                  At home, she dropped off her sneakers and light jacket, then plunked down in front of her laptop. She quickly checked the sequencer’s progress online, but everything was still progressing normally. No groundbreaking new results or finds, this thing had a monster genome, but it was mostly boring. They would have proper results soon. That night, Anna’s dreams were full of the day’s worries. It wasn’t the virus, but the day had been a lot to think about.

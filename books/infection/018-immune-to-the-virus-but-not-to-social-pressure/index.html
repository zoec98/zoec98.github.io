                                                                                                                                                    Chapter 18: Immune to the Virus, but not to Social Pressure

                                                                                                                                                    • Immune To The Virus, But Not To Social Pressure

                                                                                                                                                    Immune To The Virus, But Not To Social Pressure#

                                                                                                                                                    The virus had not rewritten Sonia’s mind the way it had rewritten Anna’s.

                                                                                                                                                    That did not mean she was untouched.

                                                                                                                                                    Geneva kept pressing on her from the outside.

                                                                                                                                                    Two days after her return, she stepped onto a tram in a soft blouse and an ordinary skirt and knew almost immediately she had made a mistake. Not a moral mistake. A practical one. The carriage was full enough that everyone had time to look and not so crowded that anyone had to pretend not to.

                                                                                                                                                    More fitted jackets now. More corsets. More narrow skirts forcing shorter steps and a different way of turning in place. The women around her looked composed in the new rules, even the younger ones who could not have been living under them for very long. That was what kept catching at her. Not theatricality. Ease.

                                                                                                                                                    She found a seat and opened the book she had brought for cover, but the line of women opposite her stayed in the edge of her vision. Three of them, all Changed, all tidy in the same legible way. One in dark green with gloves buttoned at the wrist. One in gray with a high collar and a hat pinned close to soft blond hair. One in navy, younger than the others, with the bright attentive face of someone still proud to be getting it right.

                                                                                                                                                    They looked at Sonia once, then again.

                                                                                                                                                    The youngest leaned toward the woman in gray and said something too low for most of the tram to hear.

                                                                                                                                                    Not too low for Sonia.

                                                                                                                                                    “She came out like that?”

                                                                                                                                                    The woman in gray did not quite smile.

                                                                                                                                                    “Perhaps no one corrected her before she left home.”

                                                                                                                                                    The one in green glanced down at Sonia’s ungloved hands.

                                                                                                                                                    “Poor thing.”

                                                                                                                                                    That was worse than open contempt would have been. Sonia kept her eyes on the page in front of her and felt heat rise into her face anyway.

                                                                                                                                                    At the next stop a man got on, broad-shouldered, middle-aged, carrying a leather briefcase. He paused beside the women, took in the row, and then looked at Sonia. Not with desire. Not even with much curiosity. Assessment, then polite disappointment, as if she had arrived underdressed to a formal lunch and made the whole carriage fractionally less orderly to look at.

                                                                                                                                                    He moved on without offering his seat to anyone.

                                                                                                                                                    The woman in navy followed him with her eyes, then let them return to Sonia with almost pitying composure.

                                                                                                                                                    “A little structure helps,” she said after a moment, not quite addressing Sonia directly and not quite avoiding it either. “One feels so much better.”

                                                                                                                                                    Sonia raised her eyes at last.

                                                                                                                                                    “I’m comfortable, thank you.”

                                                                                                                                                    The answer came out cooler than she had intended.

                                                                                                                                                    The woman in gray answered for the group.

                                                                                                                                                    “Comfort is not really the point.”

                                                                                                                                                    No one around them reacted. A year earlier the exchange would have drawn stares. Now it dissolved into the ordinary sound of the tram, the rails underfoot, the stop bell, the rustle of tailored skirts and coats.

                                                                                                                                                    Sonia looked back at her book and did not turn the page for three stops.

                                                                                                                                                    By the time she got off, she felt strangely unfinished. As if she had gone out of the house missing an obvious layer and only discovered it when everyone else politely chose to notice.

                                                                                                                                                    That evening Anna said nothing sharp about Sonia’s clothes.

                                                                                                                                                    That made it worse.

                                                                                                                                                    They met for coffee after Anna had spent several hours with the first batch of data from Cheng’s files. Anna arrived in a fitted cream jacket over a pale blouse with fine pintucks at the throat and a dark skirt cut narrow enough to keep every step small and exact. Julien had clearly been involved somewhere in the result. Not because the outfit was flamboyant. Because it was thoughtful. It arranged her.

                                                                                                                                                    Anna kissed Sonia’s cheek, sat carefully, and gave her one quick glance that lingered just long enough to register the soft blouse, the uncorseted line, the careless ease.

                                                                                                                                                    Then she said only, “You look tired.”

                                                                                                                                                    Sonia almost laughed.

                                                                                                                                                    “I was underdressed on the tram this morning, if that’s what you mean.”

                                                                                                                                                    Anna’s expression changed in the smallest way.

                                                                                                                                                    “Ah.”

                                                                                                                                                    “Ah?”

                                                                                                                                                    Anna lowered her eyes briefly to her cup.

                                                                                                                                                    “People can be unkind when they think they are helping.”

                                                                                                                                                    That should have been comforting. Instead it put Sonia right back on the tram, feeling those three women’s attention pass over her like fingertips checking a hem for flaws.

                                                                                                                                                    “You think they were helping.”

                                                                                                                                                    Anna looked up.

                                                                                                                                                    “I think they believed they were.”

                                                                                                                                                    The distinction irritated Sonia because it was fair.

                                                                                                                                                    She blew on her coffee and watched a woman near the window settle into her chair while the man beside her adjusted the fall of her scarf without being asked. The woman thanked him under her breath and leaned into the correction as if it had relieved a small strain she had not wanted to manage alone.

                                                                                                                                                    “It keeps happening,” Sonia said.

                                                                                                                                                    “What does?”

                                                                                                                                                    “I keep waiting for people to look oppressed.”

                                                                                                                                                    Anna went very still.

                                                                                                                                                    Sonia hated herself a little for saying it out loud, but there it was now.

                                                                                                                                                    “And they don’t,” she said. “They look… settled. Supported. Annoyingly well put together.”

                                                                                                                                                    Anna’s mouth softened.

                                                                                                                                                    “Sometimes they are.”

                                                                                                                                                    Sonia looked at her sharply.

                                                                                                                                                    “That’s not a defense.”

                                                                                                                                                    “No,” Anna said quietly. “It isn’t.”

                                                                                                                                                    For a second they sat with that.

                                                                                                                                                    Outside, a tram slid past in a wash of reflected light.

                                                                                                                                                    Anna rested her fingertips against her saucer.

                                                                                                                                                    “You can hate what was done and still notice what people build inside it,” she said. “Those things are not mutually exclusive.”

                                                                                                                                                    The sentence stayed with Sonia long after they parted.

                                                                                                                                                    The next morning she dragged the Shanghai purchases out from the back of her wardrobe.

                                                                                                                                                    The Gowbao Yoohoo blouse looked even more ridiculous in her hands than it had in the boutique. The corset looked worse. An instrument. An argument made in fabric and boning. She stood in her underwear staring at both for so long that the room cooled around her.

                                                                                                                                                    This is tactical, she told herself.

                                                                                                                                                    It was tactical. She had work to do. She needed freedom to move through the city without inviting comment every time she stepped onto a tram or into a café. Conformity was camouflage. She already knew that.

                                                                                                                                                    That did not explain why her pulse had begun to quicken.

                                                                                                                                                    The corset closed around her with humiliating familiarity. Busk. Laces. Tighten. Adjust. The blouse followed, then the underskirt, then the outer layer that made the whole thing legible instead of merely restrictive. By the time she pinned her hair and stepped into shoes with just enough heel to alter her center of gravity, the mirror had stopped reflecting a woman making a compromise and started reflecting a woman who looked as if she understood the city again.

                                                                                                                                                    She hated the relief first.

                                                                                                                                                    Then she hated how incomplete hatred felt.

                                                                                                                                                    On the tram no one looked twice at her.

                                                                                                                                                    That was its own kind of narcotic.

                                                                                                                                                    The same middle space opened around her that she had seen granted to other women: not exactly respect, not exactly protection, but a willing social ease. A man rose almost at once when she stepped into the aisle near him.

                                                                                                                                                    “Please, miss.”

                                                                                                                                                    He said it with a nod that carried expectation inside the courtesy. Sonia felt the old training come up through her body before she had time to resist it. She lowered her gaze, bent in the smallest curtsy, and thanked him properly.

                                                                                                                                                    “Thank you, Sir.”

                                                                                                                                                    There.

                                                                                                                                                    Done.

                                                                                                                                                    She sat with her knees pressed neatly together because the underskirt required it and because it was easier not to fight the clothing once she was already inside it. The carriage moved. The corset held her upright. She could feel the fabric under her arms, the pressure at her waist, the tiny careful balance required every time the tram shifted over a curve. A woman across from her gave her a faint approving smile and went back to her own thoughts.

                                                                                                                                                    Sonia stared out the window.

                                                                                                                                                    The exchange had taken seconds. It should have meant nothing. Instead it sat warm and ugly under her ribs. Not the seat itself. The little current of recognition that ran through the moment. You are dressed properly. You are behaving properly. You may belong here.

                                                                                                                                                    That calm did not look fake on other women.

                                                                                                                                                    That was the problem.

                                                                                                                                                    By the time she stepped off near the lakeside, the movements had come back fully. The smaller stride. The hand checking the skirt before sitting. The slight turn of the shoulders when making room for someone to pass. Nothing dramatic. Nothing she had to think through. Shanghai had settled into her body deeper than she wanted to admit.

                                                                                                                                                    The café had added more privacy cabins along one side since her return. Women used them casually now, slipping in and out with the matter-of-fact rhythm of people adapting to infrastructure rather than ritual. Sonia watched one woman disappear into a cabin with a paper cup and emerge a minute later smoothing her gloves back into place. No spectacle. No shame. Just process.

                                                                                                                                                    Inside, the room carried the same pressure as the tram but in a softer register. Women sat straighter. Men ordered more often. Little courtesies accumulated until they began to look like a whole social system instead of separate choices. Sonia ordered coffee and took a seat near the window.

                                                                                                                                                    At the next table two women about her age were discussing a friend who had apparently refused to start dressing properly for work.

                                                                                                                                                    “She keeps saying she wants to be taken seriously for her mind,” one said.

                                                                                                                                                    The other stirred her drink.

                                                                                                                                                    “Then she should stop insisting on looking unfinished.”

                                                                                                                                                    Neither woman sounded cruel. That was what made the line stay with Sonia.

                                                                                                                                                    Unfinished.

                                                                                                                                                    The word hooked somewhere deep. She looked down at her own gloved hands around the cup. Finished, then. Correctly assembled. Socially legible. The thought should have revolted her. It did, a little. It also reached something more embarrassing than revolt. A private slackening. The tired pleasure of not having to argue with the room.

                                                                                                                                                    Later, in the market, the same sensation sharpened.

                                                                                                                                                    The vendors were mostly women. Not masked, not silent, just brisk and competent behind orderly displays of produce, flowers, household goods, and bread. Sonia bought pears from an older woman in a navy jacket with reinforced stays visible under the front seam.

                                                                                                                                                    “Beautiful blouse,” the vendor said while weighing the fruit. “Shanghai cut?”

                                                                                                                                                    “Yes.”

                                                                                                                                                    “I thought so. It suits you.”

                                                                                                                                                    Sonia should have brushed it off.

                                                                                                                                                    Instead she said, “Thank you.”

                                                                                                                                                    The answer came out small. Almost pleased.

                                                                                                                                                    She took the pears and moved on annoyed with herself, more annoyed when she caught her reflection in a shop window and saw exactly what the woman had meant. The clothes suited her. Not in spite of what they did to her body. Because of it. The straightened posture. The disciplined line. The way the blouse and skirt turned every movement deliberate.

                                                                                                                                                    She stopped in front of the glass longer than she meant to.

                                                                                                                                                    This was not conversion. It was worse, in some ways. Conversion would have been a clean story, virus and surrender and altered values. What she had instead was friction. Resistance in the head. Appetite elsewhere. A growing suspicion that belonging, restraint, and the quiet choreography of feminine dependence might answer some need she had spent half her life pretending not to have.

                                                                                                                                                    She went home with the pears in one hand and the city’s approval still clinging to her skin like scent.

                                                                                                                                                    By evening the corset had left shallow marks at her waist.

                                                                                                                                                    She stood in front of the mirror after undressing and touched them lightly.

                                                                                                                                                    The apartment was quiet. No one was there to praise her. No one to correct her. No one to tell her how much better she looked when properly arranged.

                                                                                                                                                    She thought of the tram seat offered with that little nod. Of the approving glance from the woman opposite. Of the vendor saying it suits you. Of Anna speaking about settlement in a voice so calm it was almost impossible to call delusion.

                                                                                                                                                    Then she thought of taking the corset back out tomorrow morning instead of shoving it away again.

                                                                                                                                                    That thought did not feel like defeat.

                                                                                                                                                    It felt dangerous for subtler reasons.
